# CSC-ANE-01 — Foundational Anesthesia Monitoring
Companion to TS-ANE-103 · Benchmark course, gateway to the Anesthesia Academy · ~40 min + supervised cases · Role banner: you SUPPORT the anesthetist. You never adjust the vaporizer, APL, or drugs. Your outputs are observations, records, and reports. Everything below teaches you to make those outputs excellent.

## 1. Purpose
Anesthesia monitoring is not writing numbers every five minutes — that's anesthesia RECORDING, and a clipboard could do it. Monitoring is continuously answering one question: *does this patient's whole picture make sense right now?* The monitor supports patient monitoring; it does not replace it. This course builds the loop you'll run hundreds of times: hands and eyes on the patient → numbers → trend → "does it fit?" → record → report what doesn't fit, at the urgency it deserves.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge — enough physiology to reason with
- **Circulation:** cardiac output = HR × stroke volume. Anesthetics vasodilate and depress the heart dose-dependently → deeper usually means lower pressure. Blood pressure is the easy proxy; PERFUSION (blood actually reaching tissues) is the goal — which is why gums, CRT, and pulse quality stay in your loop even with a BP monitor running.
- **Ventilation vs oxygenation — two different failures:** ventilation is moving air (CO2 out); oxygenation is loading O2 into blood. A patient on 100% oxygen can hypoventilate for a long time with a PERFECT SpO2 — the CO2 climbs while the pulse ox smiles. That's why EtCO2 and SpO2 are cross-checks, not substitutes.
- **EtCO2 needs three systems working:** CO2 must be produced (metabolism), delivered to the lungs (circulation!), and exhaled (ventilation + open circuit). A collapsing EtCO2 with unchanged breathing is a CIRCULATION alarm as much as a breathing one.
- **ECG shows electricity, not squeeze:** an organized trace with no pulse is possible and catastrophic — which is why your finger on a pulse outranks the green line.
- **Temperature is an anesthetic:** cold patients need less agent, wake slower, clot worse. A falling temp with a "conveniently stable" patient is deepening anesthesia in disguise.

## 3. Equipment (what each sensor actually measures)
Pulse oximeter (light absorption through tissue → % hemoglobin saturated + a plethysmograph wave whose QUALITY is the number's credibility score); capnograph (CO2 in sampled breath → waveform + EtCO2 + inspired CO2); BP (oscillometric cuff cycles, or Doppler crystal + sphygmomanometer — cuff width ~40% of limb circumference; narrow reads high, wide reads low); ECG (three electrodes, standard placement per house convention, contact gel); thermometer (continuous probe or serial rectal); YOUR SENSES (the only multi-parameter device with judgment). The anesthesia record sheet and a working pen are monitoring equipment — treat them so.

## 4–5. Patient & operator preparation
Before induction, know YOUR patient: age, breed risks (brachycephalic airway; sighthound recovery), premeds given and their expected signatures (an alpha-2 bradycardia you expect is information; one you didn't expect is a question), baseline TPR, the case's ALERT BANDS (per CRC-003 defaults or the anesthetist's stated case-specific numbers — ask: "what are my report thresholds today?"). Your station: where you can SEE chest, bag, monitor, and the anesthetist without moving your feet; record sheet on a board; the patient's head and the reservoir bag in your natural eye-line. Probes placed per house convention: SpO2 tongue (or lip/ear/prepuce/vulva alternates when tongue fails), ECG per convention, cuff sized against the limb — do the 40% eyeball check every time.

## 6. The procedure — the five-minute loop (run it identically, every cycle, entire case)
**Beat 1 — HANDS AND EYES FIRST (before looking at any screen).** DO: chest excursion (moving? symmetric? effort?), reservoir bag (moving with the chest? how full?), MM color + CRT, palpate pulse (strong? matches the ECG beep?), eye position/palpebral/jaw tone per the anesthetist's depth-check routine (you observe and report; depth CALLS are theirs), temperature per schedule. WHY: the patient is the primary instrument; every artifact-vs-real decision you'll ever make starts with this beat already done. EXPECT: ~30 seconds once practiced. COMMON ERROR: screen-first monitoring — numbers hypnotize; the beat order is the antidote. STOP: bag not moving, no palpable pulse, blue/white gums = say it LOUD now — these are not "note at next interval" findings.
**Beat 2 — NUMBERS.** DO: read HR/rhythm, SpO2 + pleth quality (clean waves = credible; ragged = suspect), EtCO2 + waveform shape (square-ish? baseline at zero?), BP, temp, agent %/O2 flow as displayed. COMMON ERROR: recording SpO2 without glancing at the pleth — a number without its credibility score.
**Beat 3 — TREND.** DO: compare against the last 3–4 entries, not just the last one; ask "what direction is each number walking?" A MAP of 62 that was 78→71→66 is a different fact than a steady 62. WHY: trends are where anesthesia problems live; single values are where artifacts live.
**Beat 4 — "DOES IT FIT?"** DO: run the cross-check — do the numbers agree with each other AND with your Beat-1 hands? HR up + BP down + pale gums = a coherent (bad) story: report now. SpO2 88 + ragged pleth + pink gums + good pulse = an incoherent story: suspect the probe, fix it, AND say what you're doing. WHY: this beat is the whole difference between a monitor and a scribe.
**Beat 5 — RECORD.** DO: chart the cycle's values at the interval, draw the trend lines, stamp events as they happen (position change, surgeon milestone, drug given by anesthetist, probe repositioned, ANY report you made and when). Gaps stay gaps — a blank cell is honest; a backfilled guess is fiction in a legal record. COMMON ERROR: batch-charting three cycles from memory during a quiet stretch.
**Beat 6 — REPORT (when triggered).** DO: use the case's bands and the trend rule — crossing a band, WALKING toward a band, waveform loss/shape change, any Beat-1 physical change, any equipment doubt. Format: finding + trend + credibility note + urgency. "MAP 58, down from 70 over fifteen minutes, cuff rechecked and placement good — this looks real, and it's still drifting." WHY: that sentence gives the anesthetist everything: value, slope, artifact-screen, and your read. COMMON ERROR: silent troubleshooting (fixing the probe for four minutes while a real desaturation runs) — the rule is REPORT-WHILE-CHECKING, never instead of.

## 7. Tactile & sensory medicine
The reservoir bag is your remote hand on the lungs: its excursion size is tidal volume's shadow; its rhythm is RR; a bag that stops is apnea NOW (say it, don't verify it to death). A pulse under your finger: full and pushing vs thin and tapping — pressure's texture; practice calibrating your finger against the BP monitor every case (finger says weak + monitor says 55 = your finger just got smarter). Gum color changes are subtle under OR lights — compare against YOUR baseline check, same spot, same lighting. The capnograph's sound (where enabled) lets your ears monitor while your eyes chart; learn the pitch change of a falling EtCO2. Cold: the patient's ears and paws chill FIRST — your hand on an ear between cycles is a free leading indicator.

## 8. It doesn't fit — now what? (the artifact-vs-real drills)
**SpO2 drops:** pleth check FIRST. Clean wave = believe it: report immediately with gum/pulse corroboration. Ragged wave = probe (position, cold tongue, pressure) — announce + reposition + recheck; if the new site agrees with the bad number, it was never artifact. NEVER silence a saturation alarm on the theory it's artifact — test the theory out loud.
**EtCO2 flatlines:** the big one. Hands on: chest moving? bag moving? circuit connected at every joint? sampling line kinked/water-logged? Say "EtCO2 just went flat — checking circuit — chest IS/ISN'T moving" AS you check. Flat + no chest movement = apnea/arrest pathway — the anesthetist owns the response; your loud early words own the timeline. (SpO2 stays falsely fine for minutes on oxygen — do not let it talk you down.)
**EtCO2 climbing slowly:** hypoventilation pattern (common, drug-related) vs rebreathing (baseline off zero — absorbent/valves). Check the BASELINE first: it sorts the two in one glance. Report the trend with the baseline fact attached.
**BP reading absurd:** cuff size/site/limb-motion recheck, one repeat cycle, palpate the pulse yourself — then report BOTH the number and your verification ("oscillometric says 40 but femoral feels strong; cuff rechecked; repeating — wanted you to know while I verify").
**ECG chaos, patient fine:** electrode contact (dried gel, slipped clip) — fix, report what you found; the discipline is that ECG weirdness gets EXPLAINED, never shrugged at, because the one time it's real matters more than the nine times it's a dry clip.
**HR/rhythm changes that ARE real** (bradycardia drifting down, sudden tachycardia, irregularity with pulse deficits): these are report-now, with the premed context you memorized ("HR 48, was 70 at start — she had dexmedetomidine, but the slope is steady down").
**Bag/chest disagreement** (bag moving, chest barely; or effort against the circuit): circuit/airway question — report immediately; do not adjust anything.
**The meta-rule:** you may PHYSICALLY CHECK (probes, leads, cuffs, line tracing, sampling line) on your own initiative WHILE reporting; you may never treat, adjust the machine, or decide-not-to-mention. When in doubt, the sentence "I'm probably wrong, but look at this" has saved more patients than every monitor combined.

## 9. Real patients
**Cats:** small tidal volumes make bag-reading subtle — stand closer; hypothermia arrives fast and drives everything; SpO2 tongue probes fail more (small tongue, compression artifact) — know your alternate sites. **Brachycephalics:** the airway is the story start to finish; recovery vigilance doctrine (they obstruct AFTER extubation) begins in your case notes now. **Geriatrics:** less reserve — trends move faster from "fine" to "not"; report earlier on the slope. **Pediatrics:** glucose and temperature fragility; minimal-drape heat loss is YOUR watch. **Sighthounds:** lean bodies chill and recover differently; the anesthetist will brief — your job is knowing that a briefing exists to ask for. **The dental (longest cases):** water + time = the hypothermia case study; temp trend is the headline number by hour two. **Emergency/compromised patients:** bands are set case-specific and tighter; ask for them explicitly at induction — "what are my numbers today?" marks you as exactly the monitor the academy wants.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Screen-first monitoring** — the hypnotized clipboard. Prevention: Beat 1 is muscle memory before your first live case (drilled in sim). 2. **Recording without reading** — perfect penmanship, no idea the MAP has fallen 20 points over four entries. Prevention: Beat 3's compare-against-three rule. 3. **Silent troubleshooting** — the four quiet minutes that become the incident report's centerpiece. 4. **Artifact-calling by preference** — "probably the probe" because that's the comfortable world. Prevention: the test-the-theory-out-loud rule. 5. **Backfilled records** — fiction with a time stamp. 6. **Threshold worship** — waiting at 61 for a band set at 60 while the slope screams. 7. **Speaking too softly** to interrupt — the report that wasn't heard didn't happen; closed-loop it ("did you catch that MAP?").

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: Narrate your first cases aloud quietly — anesthetists correct narration in real time and you learn triple-speed. · Put your watch/timer where the record sheet is; cycles drift late during surgeon-interesting moments, which are exactly the moments monitoring matters. · Calibrate your finger against the monitor every case (the pulse-vs-number game) — in a year your finger will be the best sensor in the room. · During quiet stretches, pre-read the NEXT likely event (closing = lightening; moving to rads = disconnection risk) and stage your attention. · Learn each anesthetist's briefing style and ask for bands up front; being briefed well is a skill you can cause. BANNED: touching the vaporizer/APL/flowmeter uninstructed, ever, including "just turning it down while she's clearly light" (report instead — depth is a prescription); silencing any alarm without a stated reason; "she's fine, she's just deep" as a self-issued diagnosis; leaving your station without a named replacement watching ("thirty seconds" is how recovery deaths start).

## 14. Technique variation & house standard
**Outcome requirements:** the patient is continuously watched by someone who would notice change; the record reflects what happened; anything that does not fit is spoken aloud at the right urgency.

**CVAH house standards** `[HOUSE]`: hands and eyes before screens each cycle (reason: monitors fail, patients don't lie); report while checking, never instead of checking (reason: silent troubleshooting is how deterioration gets missed); assistants never touch vaporizer, APL, or flows (reason: scope — depth is a prescription).

**Genuinely variable:** the order within the hands-on beat; which alternate SpO2 site you try first; charting continuously versus at the interval mark; how you phrase a report — **the content matters, the script does not**; where you stand, so long as you can see chest, bag, monitor and anesthetist.

## 15. What may vary without being wrong
> Do not fail a learner for: a different but complete observation sequence · reporting something that turns out to be artifact — **over-reporting at this stage is the correct error to make and must never be scored as a fault** · asking the anesthetist to confirm a threshold · charting a limitation instead of a value they could not obtain.

## 16. Precision audit
`[EVIDENCE]`: the cuff width ≈ 40% of limb circumference rule of thumb, applied by eye.
`[GUIDE]`: the five-beat loop structure · "~30 seconds" for the hands-on beat.
`[HOUSE]`: patient-before-monitor · report-while-checking · no dials.
`[SAFETY]`: a flat capnograph means hands on patient and circuit immediately · never silence an alarm without a stated reason · never leave an anesthetized patient unwatched.
**All numeric alert thresholds live in CRC-003 and are deliberately absent from this course** — it teaches you to use the card, not to memorise numbers a veterinarian has not yet approved.
