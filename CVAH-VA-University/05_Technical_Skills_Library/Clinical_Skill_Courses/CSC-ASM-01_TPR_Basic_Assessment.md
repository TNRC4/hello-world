# CSC-ASM-01 — TPR & Basic Patient Assessment
Companion to TS-ASM-001/002 · Benchmark course · ~25 min + daily practice

## 1. Purpose
TPR is the hospital's most-repeated clinical measurement and the foundation of every trend that ever catches a deteriorating patient. The skill is producing numbers a DVM can ACT on — which means accurate, honestly-annotated, and delivered at the right urgency. A fabricated or sloppy TPR is worse than none: it launders a sick patient into a stable-looking chart.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
Temperature: core heat; fever (set-point up: infection/inflammation) vs hyperthermia (heat in faster than out: stress, heatstroke) feel identical on the thermometer — context distinguishes, which is why you report, not interpret. Pulse: each heartbeat should push a pressure wave to the femoral artery; a heard-beat-without-felt-pulse (deficit) means non-perfusing beats — always reportable. Respiration: one cycle = in AND out; CHARACTER (effort, abdominal push, noise) is earlier warning than rate. Stress artifact: handling raises RR and HR within seconds — sequence your exam around that fact.

## 3. Equipment
Digital thermometer + probe covers + lube; watch/clock with seconds (the wall clock counts); stethoscope that fits YOUR ears (angle earpieces forward); your eyes — the most-used instrument in this course.

## 4–5. Patient & operator preparation
Order of operations is the technique: EYES FIRST (RR + character from a distance), HANDS SECOND (pulse + auscultation), THERMOMETER LAST (the most annoying part goes at the end so it can't contaminate the other numbers). You at the kennel front or table side; patient wherever it's calmest — a resting kennel RR beats a table RR every time. For the hands-on portion: standing or sternal restraint per CSC-RES-01/02, minimal version.

## 6. The procedure
**Step 1 — Respiration from a distance.** DO: before opening the kennel, count chest rises 15 s × 4 (or 30 × 2 if steady); watch the SHAPE of breathing: smooth chest rise vs abdominal push; listen for noise (stertor = snore-like, stridor = high whistle); note posture (elbows-out orthopnea, neck extended). WHY: this is the only uncontaminated respiratory data you'll get. EXPECT: a resting rhythm you can tap along to. COMMON ERROR: counting each chest movement as one (doubles the rate); counting a panting dog and charting it as RR (chart "panting," count later or note unobtainable). RECOVERY: lost count = start over; never round a guess upward into the chart. STOP: laboured breathing at rest = report NOW, before the rest of the TPR — the exam can wait, the finding can't.
**Step 2 — Pulse and heart together.** DO: stethoscope over the left chest at/behind the elbow while two fingers (never your thumb — it has its own pulse) find the femoral artery: inner thigh, near where leg meets body, in the muscle groove — press lightly, then lighter. Count HR 15–60 s; feel simultaneously: does every beat arrive at the leg? Note rhythm (regular? regularly-irregular with breathing — normal in dogs — vs chaotic) and pulse QUALITY (strong/weak/bounding, described not diagnosed). WHY: the simultaneous feel is what catches deficits; quality is free perfusion data. EXPECT: dog 70–120 (size-dependent), cat 160–220 in clinic; sinus arrhythmia in relaxed dogs is a normal finding worth knowing by ear. COMMON ERROR: pressing the femoral so hard you occlude it ("no pulse!" on a healthy dog); counting a purring cat's HR by hope (chart the limitation per the honest-note rule). RECOVERY: can't hear the heart (obese/deep-chested) → slide cranioventrally, use the femoral count as the number and say so. STOP: pulse deficit, chaotic rhythm, or bounding-then-absent quality = report-now findings.
**Step 3 — Temperature.** DO: cover + lube; lift tail gently; insert 1–2 cm with a slight tilt so the tip contacts the rectal wall; STAY WITH IT until the beep; read, cover off into trash, wipe. WHY: leaving a thermometer unattended in a standing patient is how thermometers (and dignity) break. EXPECT: 100.0–102.5°F typical adult band (CRC-001 is the authority). COMMON ERROR: charting a stress-bumped 102.9 without context, or repeating a shocking value hoping it changes — one honest repeat to rule out technique is fine; serial re-takes hunting a better number is data-laundering. STOP: rectal pain/resistance, fresh blood on cover, recent rectal surgery per record → don't force; note and report.
**Step 4 — The free extras while hands are on.** DO: lift a lip: gum COLOR (pink / pale / white / blue / brick red / yellow), MOISTURE (slick vs tacky); press and release for CRT (<2 s); quick skin tent over the shoulders; mentation word (BAR/QAR/lethargic/obtunded — pick one, per the ladder); note anything your hands found (heat in a joint, a mass, wet fur at a catheter). WHY: thirty extra seconds makes this a basic assessment, not a number errand. COMMON ERROR: "MM pink" charted through a closed mouth from memory.
**Step 5 — Chart NOW and route by urgency.** DO: values + descriptors + time + initials at the kennel/table; compare against CRC-001 bands AND against the patient's last values (the trend is the point); out-of-band or trend-adverse → SBAR-V now; borderline → note-and-flag at the next huddle. COMMON ERROR: batch-charting at shift's end from memory (integrity cliff); reporting only the number without the trend ("temp is 103.1 — up from 101.8 at 8 AM" is twice the information).

## 7. Tactile medicine
A strong femoral pulse taps your fingers like a firm fingertip drum; weak feels like the drum moved a room away; bounding SLAPS then vanishes (that snap-emptiness pattern is worth reporting in those words). The normal dog's sinus arrhythmia speeds with inhale, slows with exhale — feel it once on a calm patient and arrhythmia-vs-normal gets much easier. A dehydrated patient's skin tent doesn't SNAP back; it strolls. Tacky gums drag your fingertip; slick gums let it skate.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**Squirmy patient corrupting every count:** better restraint (CSC-RES), not a worse number; if unattainable calmly, chart the limitation and the pieces you trust ("RR 32 resting pre-handling; HR unobtainable — struggling; femoral strong"). **Numbers that don't fit the patient** (HR 60 in a stressed cat; T 96.8 in a bright dog): re-take ONCE for technique (thermometer contact? counting error?), then report the discrepancy itself — "the number looks wrong for the patient" is a legitimate, valuable report. **Equipment doubt:** thermometer reading absurdly (86°F) = battery/technique; verify on yourself-adjacent logic (a second thermometer), tag the bad unit out. **You forgot the pre-handling RR:** own it — wait five quiet minutes and count again, or chart "post-handling" honestly; never back-fill the resting number you wish you'd taken.

## 9. Real patients
**Cats in clinic:** everything runs high on stress — the trend across visits and the character findings outweigh single numbers; count RR through the carrier door on arrival (gold data, free). **Brachycephalics:** baseline noise is real but NEVER dismiss new noise as "just being a bulldog" — compare to THEIR baseline. **Puppies/kittens:** higher normal everything; wiggle = count longer windows. **Geriatrics:** arthritis makes tail-lifts unkind — slow hands; low-normal temps common. **Thick coats:** auscultation through undercoat mutes sounds — part the coat, press firmer, or trust the femoral. **Post-op patients:** shivering wrecks auscultation and RR counts — chart context; temperature is the headline number in recovery. **Dyspneic patients:** the TPR is ABBREVIATED by design — RR/character from distance, gums if trivially accessible, EVERYTHING else deferred; minimal-handling doctrine outranks completeness.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **RR after wrestling** — the artifact chart. 2. **Half-cycle doubling.** 3. **Thumb on the femoral** — counting your own pulse. 4. **Thermometer walk-away.** 5. **Memory charting at shift end.** 6. **Serial re-takes hunting a nicer number.** 7. **"WNL" as a reflex** — normal is a finding you MEASURED, not a mood. 8. **Reporting numbers naked** — without trend, character, or context words that make them actionable.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: Kennel-front RR counts on your ward walk cost nothing and build the trend chart that catches the 2 AM decliner. · Tap your foot to the heartbeat while counting — rhythm anomalies pop out. · Warm the stethoscope head in your palm (cold metal = flinch = artifact). · Say numbers aloud as you chart them; transposition errors die in your own ears. · The patient's LAST TPR is the first thing you read, not the last. BANNED: axillary temps charted as rectal without labeling; "she looks fine, call it 101" (fabrication, the terminable kind); pinching gums hard for a "better" CRT.
