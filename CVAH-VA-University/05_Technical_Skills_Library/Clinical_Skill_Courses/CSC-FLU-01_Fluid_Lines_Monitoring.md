# CSC-FLU-01 — Fluid Line Setup, Pumps & Line Monitoring
Companion to TS-FLU-001/002 · Benchmark course · ~30 min + lab

## 1. Purpose
Fluid therapy is continuous treatment delivered by equipment YOU set up and watch. The DVM chooses fluid and rate; the CVT verifies; but the line's actual behavior — air, kinks, infiltration, free-flow, the pump quietly pushing fluid under the skin — lives or dies on assistant discipline. This course makes you the person whose lines simply work, and whose hourly checks catch problems while they're still small.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
Gravity and resistance run everything: fluid falls from bag to patient through a tube whose flow you meter (roller clamp) or a pump meters for you. Drip sets are calibrated: macro 10 or 15 gtt/mL, micro 60 gtt/mL — the number is printed on the package and it changes all your math. Pumps push at a set rate AGAINST resistance and count VOLUME PUSHED — they do not know whether it entered a vein or a subcutaneous pocket; that knowledge is your palpating hand's job. Air: small bubbles are a bigger deal in smaller patients; the discipline is air-free priming regardless. Additives: any bag with drugs added (KCl the classic) is CVT/DVM-verified, big-labeled, and pump-only — potassium free-flowed is a lethal error class.

## 3. Equipment
Ordered bag (inspect: squeeze test for leaks, clarity — cloudy/particulate = discard/report, expiry); admin set matched to patient size per order/house rule; extension set/T-port; labels; pump or gravity pole; tape.

## 4–5. Preparation
Verify order completely: fluid TYPE, RATE, VOLUME/duration, additives (verified by whom?). Stage at the pole: bag hung, set unwrapped, clamp location found and CLOSED before spiking. Operator position: bag at eye level for spiking (spike port pointing down at your face is how fluids end up in your eyes), drip chamber at eye level for filling.

## 6. The procedure
**Step 1 — Spike.** DO: close roller clamp; peel the bag's port cover; remove spike guard; one firm straight push of the spike fully into the port — no twisting wobble, hands never touching the spike shaft. WHY: the spike is a sterile pathway; wobble = leaks and contamination. EXPECT: firm seat, no drips at the junction. COMMON ERROR: spiking with clamp open (fluid races through, air-locked chamber chaos); half-seated spike weeping at the shoulder. RECOVERY: contaminated or half-seated spike = new set; sets are cheap, line infections aren't.
**Step 2 — Chamber.** DO: squeeze the drip chamber until fluid fills 1/3–1/2. WHY: you need visible falling drops AND an air gap (full chamber can't count drops; empty chamber sends air down-line). COMMON ERROR: overfill — recover by inverting the bag+chamber and squeezing air back up.
**Step 3 — Prime.** DO: open clamp SLOWLY; walk the fluid column down the line watching it; tap junctions/ports as fluid passes (bubbles cling there); fill to the very end; close clamp; cap the end sterile. EXPECT: one continuous glassy column — hold the line against the light and run your eyes down its whole length; champagne trains of micro-bubbles fail the inspection for small patients. COMMON ERROR: priming at full-blast (turbulence = bubbles), or priming "most of the way." RECOVERY: bubbles present → clamp, coil-tap them upward into the chamber, or run further fluid through; never decide they're "small enough" for a kitten. STOP: n/a.
**Step 4 — Pump loading (pump lines).** DO: ROLLER CLAMP CLOSED FIRST — say it aloud, it's the free-flow catechism — door open, set seated per THIS model's channel (orientation arrows matter; a backwards set on some models flows uncontrolled), door closed, clamp open, THEN program: rate (mL/h) and VTBI verified against the written order by CVT/DVM per policy; make the display read back the order. WHY: the clamp-before-door habit is the only universal free-flow defense across every pump model ever made. EXPECT: pump primes/tests then holds. COMMON ERROR: mL total vs mL/h confusion at entry (a 420 in the rate field is a bolus disaster) — the read-back-against-order step exists for this. STOP: any doubt about rate math = verify before start, not after.
**Step 5 — Connect.** DO: aseptic cap-off, connect to the catheter's T-port with a firm twist; open/start; watch the FIRST minute: drips falling at the expected rhythm (gravity: your gtt/min math — e.g., 60 mL/h on a 15 gtt set = 15 gtt/min = one drop per 4 seconds), site flat, patient undisturbed. Secure a strain loop of line to the limb wrap: tension arrives at tape, not catheter. Label line and bag: date/time started, set type, initials. COMMON ERROR: connecting an unprimed "just a little air" line; no slack loop (first stretch pulls the catheter). STOP: swelling or wet at the site in minute one = stop, assess per CSC-IVC-01 §8.
**Step 6 — The hourly check (the actual job).** DO, in the same order every time: (1) PUMP/DRIP — running? rate on display matches ordered? drops actually falling? (2) TRACE the entire line bag→patient with your fingers — kinks, closed clamps, disconnections, chew marks, tangles around limbs; (3) SITE — palpate above the wrap: soft? cool doughy swelling? wet fur? compare limbs; (4) BAG — volume remaining vs time math (a pump claiming 200 delivered from a bag missing 40 = report NOW); (5) PATIENT — comfortable? mobile without tension? e-collar intact if flagged? Chart the check. WHY: five channels because each catches what the others miss. COMMON ERROR: the display-glance "check." STOP: any infiltration sign = stop flow, report; never "watch it another hour."
**Step 7 — Bag change.** DO: verify new bag against order (same everything, additive verification if any); close clamp; swap spike aseptically old→new in one motion; chamber/rate re-check; document volumes (remaining in old, discarded, started new) — the I/O math depends on your arithmetic. COMMON ERROR: spike touched or laid down mid-swap; volumes guessed.

## 7. Tactile medicine
A patent line under a slow flush: water through a straw. Infiltrated: bread-dough absorption — soft, wrong, quiet. Occluded: a wall. (Same three sensations as CSC-IVC-01 — they're one skill.) A healthy drip chamber has a metronome you can FEEL by watching; rate drift is visible before any alarm. The line-trace hand learns to read kinks like Braille — do it eyes-closed in the lab once; your fingers are faster than your eyes in a dark ward.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**Occlusion alarm:** trace first — kink (open it), clamp (open it), limb position (flex/extend gently: positional flow = re-tape/splint decision to report); then site assessment; NEVER respond by raising the pressure limit or silencing-and-walking. **Air-in-line alarm:** clamp, disconnect at the port per protocol, re-prime the segment or replace the set; the forbidden move is opening the door and flicking bubbles toward the patient. **Rate behind schedule:** don't "catch up" by doubling the rate — that's a prescribing act; report the shortfall, the DVM adjusts. **Pump alarming repeatedly, no cause found in 2 min:** swap pump per CVT, tag the offender OUT OF SERVICE with a note — nuisance-alarm pumps never quietly return to rotation. **Bag ran dry unnoticed:** line has air and the catheter may clot — clamp, report, expect re-prime + flush assessment; then fix the system cause (VTBI alarm not set? check skipped?). **Patient chewed the line:** clamp both ends of the damage, assess what was lost (and whether the catheter survived), report; the e-collar conversation is now mandatory. **Fluid running but patient's limb is a balloon:** hours of sub-q delivery — stop, report NOW; log honestly which checks were signed and what they found; this event is the reason the five-channel check exists.

## 9. Real patients
**Kittens/pediatrics:** micro set (60 gtt/mL) or syringe pump per order; air discipline absolute; check frequency often ordered higher — tiny patients overload on volumes adult eyes consider rounding errors, so your bag-math is a safety system. **Cardiac patients:** the volume-sensitive class; NEVER free-flow anything; rate verification is life-critical; report ANY unexplained rate discrepancy immediately. **Cats:** line-chewers and carpal-kinkers — e-collar flags, extra slack loops, positional-occlusion expectations. **Mobile ward dogs:** entanglement engineering — line routed over the kennel door per house style, walk protocols (clamp-and-cap vs travel-pump per order). **Recumbent patients:** the down-side limb hides infiltration under body weight — palpate BOTH sides of the limb at every check. **Additive bags (KCl et al.):** pump-only, big label facing outward, YOUR verification role is confirming the label matches the order at every bag change — additives are where double-checks earn their keep.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Clamp-open pump loading** — the free-flow setup. 2. **"Mostly primed" lines.** 3. **Display-glance checks** — the pump lies by omission. 4. **Catch-up rate boosts** — prescribing by arithmetic. 5. **No strain loop.** 6. **Chamber overfill** and the lost drop-count. 7. **Guessed bag volumes** wrecking I/O math. 8. **Silencing alarms as a reflex** — alarm fatigue is trained INTO people by every un-investigated silence; break the habit before it forms.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: prime with the line's end held ABOVE the chamber then lower it — the column chases bubbles out ahead of itself. · Loop-tape a pen-width slack coil at the kennel door; door swings stop yanking catheters. · Write bag start-volume ON the bag with time — bedside math beats memory. · At every check, back-count: "bag says 400 left, started 1000 at 8 AM at 100/h — it's noon, should say 600… report." (That five-second arithmetic catches misloaded sets better than any alarm.) · Learn ONE pump deeply (your house model) rather than all pumps shallowly — the registry job aid is your friend. BANNED: flicking air toward patients; pressure-limit raises to silence occlusion alarms; potassium-added bags on gravity "just for transport"; blowing into a drip chamber to adjust it (yes, it happens; no, never).
