# CSC-IVC-01 — Cephalic IV Catheter Placement
Companion to TS-IVC-CEPH-001 · Benchmark exemplar course · ~40 min + lab · Policy gate: scope row SCOPE-004 decides whether your track is PLACER or ASSIST-AND-MAINTAIN; both tracks complete this course.

## 1. Purpose
You are installing a soft plastic tube inside a vein and making it survive hours-to-days of patient life. This is venipuncture's harder sibling: the stick is only the entry exam — threading, securing, and the first flush decide everything. A working catheter is the front door for fluids, IV meds, anesthesia, and emergencies; a "placed" catheter that's actually subcutaneous is worse than none, because everyone downstream trusts it.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
Everything from CSC-VEN-01 (same vessel), plus the geometry that rules this skill: an over-the-needle catheter is a plastic sleeve riding on a steel stylet, and the plastic tip sits 1–2 mm BEHIND the steel point. Consequence one: flash proves the STEEL is in the vein — the plastic may still be outside the wall. That's why the whole unit advances 1–2 mm after flash before anything threads. Consequence two: a catheter pulled backward over its stylet can catch the stylet's edge and shear — leaving plastic in the vein to embolize. The unit comes out together, always, forever. Third law: start DISTAL. A blown attempt low on the leg leaves everything above it usable; a blown attempt high orphans the whole vein.

## 3. Equipment
Catheter gauges (memorize the house colors against stock): 24 g cats/small; 22 g most cats and small dogs; 20 g medium-large dogs; 18 g large dogs/surgical orders. Selection logic: big enough for the job (radius⁴ — flow lives and dies on gauge), small enough for the vessel. Plus: T-port or injection cap PRIMED with saline (an unprimed T-port injects its dead-space air), 3× saline flushes, clippers with a clean cool blade, chlorhex scrub + alcohol per protocol, tape: three strips pre-torn and hanging off the table edge IN ORDER (½" under-hub strip, 1" wing strip, 1" loop strip), vetwrap, label tape written or ready (date/time/gauge/initials), sharps at elbow distance. Inspect the catheter: slide the plastic off and back on the stylet ONCE in the wrapper (breaks the seal so threading is smooth); reject any unit with a burred or fish-lipped tip; never touch the shaft.

## 4. Patient preparation
Which limb: the order may specify (surgeon preference); otherwise — no bruises, no old sites, not the leg with anything else planned. Clip GENEROUSLY: carpus to mid-forearm, around the sides — fur at the margin is a wick and a contamination mop, and a stingy clip is the classic false economy. Prep: scrub-alcohol cycles per protocol; after the final pass the entry zone is untouchable — if you must re-palpate, do it above your entry point or through a fresh alcohol swab, or re-prep. Difficulty flags that change your plan BEFORE the stick: dehydrated (pre-warm limb, expect slow flash), chondrodystrophic legs (short runway — start extra distal), cats (kink-point at the carpus — plan the tape job before the stick).

## 5. Operator preparation
Same table geometry as CSC-VEN-01: holder occludes and rolls at the elbow; you face the limb head-on, non-dominant hand applying distal traction with thumb BESIDE the vein line. Differences that matter: your dominant hand holds the catheter unit like a pencil at the hub-wings, index finger free to slide the catheter forward later WITHOUT the hand moving; the tape strips hang where your eyes can find them blind; your monologue is planned ("flash… flatten… advance… thread") — narration paces hands under stress, and your assessor will expect it.

## 6. The procedure
**Step 1 — Verify + sanity-check the order.** DO: two-identifier check PLUS gauge-vs-patient plausibility ("18 g for this 3 kg kitten?" = query, don't improvise). WHY: silently placing a "better" gauge is order deviation; placing an absurd one is harm. STOP: unresolved mismatch.
**Step 2 — Stage.** DO: everything in §3 laid out; T-port primed and capped; tape order rehearsed by touch. EXPECT: from flash to secured should never wait on a supply run. COMMON ERROR: priming the T-port "after it's in" — the classic air-push.
**Step 3 — Raise, fix, enter.** As CSC-VEN-01 steps 3–5 with two deltas: entry site is DISTAL (dorsal carpus region upward), and angle a touch shallower on entry (15–25°) because your next move is flattening further. EXPECT: flash into the catheter's hub chamber — it fills slower than a needle-hub flash; watch for it deliberately.
**Step 4 — The beat that makes or breaks it: flatten + advance the UNIT.** DO: on flash, drop the angle nearly parallel to the limb and advance the WHOLE unit 1–2 mm. Count it in your monologue. WHY: the plastic tip must enter the lumen before it can thread (see §2). EXPECT: flash continues or brightens slightly. COMMON ERROR: threading on first flash — the single most common failure in all of catheter-placement, everywhere, forever. RECOVERY: if you threaded early and it resisted — do NOT pull the catheter back over the stylet; remove the unit together, pressure, next site. STOP: n/a.
**Step 5 — Thread.** DO: stylet hand becomes a statue; index finger (or other hand) slides the catheter forward off the stylet in one smooth push to the hub. It should glide like a straw into a thick shake — light, continuous resistance, no force. WHY: the plastic follows the vessel; force means it's not in the vessel. EXPECT: hub reaches skin; slight blood ooze at hub is normal. COMMON ERROR: pushing catheter AND stylet together deeper "for security" (back-wall exit), or wiggling/rotating aggressively against resistance. RECOVERY: slight quarter-turn while threading is legal for a sticky start; ANY real resistance = out as a unit, new site. STOP: two failed placements by you = hand off, per the attempt rule.
**Step 6 — Stylet out, dam on.** DO: holder releases occlusion; your free thumb presses the skin OVER the catheter's tip (not the hub) to dam flow; stylet pulls straight out and goes DIRECTLY to sharps — it never touches the table. EXPECT: minimal blood with a good dam. COMMON ERROR: stylet parked on the table "for a second" during taping — the injury statistic in this procedure. STOP: n/a.
**Step 7 — Connect + the first flush (the truth serum).** DO: primed T-port on, firm twist; flush 1–3 mL slowly, watching and feeling THREE channels: thumb-feel (smooth, near-zero resistance?), site-look (any soft bleb rising beside the vessel?), patient-face (wince?). Optionally aspirate first — a lazy flash back confirms lumen. WHY: the flush is your only proof the PLASTIC is intravascular. A beautiful placement that blebs is a failed placement. EXPECT: effortless push; maybe a shiver of cold-saline reaction. COMMON ERROR: flushing fast ("it's fine it's fine") and missing the small bleb under fur. RECOVERY: any resistance/bleb/pain = it's not in; remove, pressure, report, restart at a new site (per your track). STOP: never flush against firm resistance — anywhere, ever.
**Step 8 — Tape architecture.** DO in order: STRIP 1 (½", sticky-up) slides UNDER the hub, wraps up and over crossing on top — this is the anchor that owns the catheter. STRIP 2 (1") flat over the wings/hub, around the limb — snug, not tight. STRIP 3 (1") captures the T-port in a gentle loop with its slack curving toward the elbow — the stress loop; a tug on the line must pull tape, not catheter. Tension test on EVERY strip: one finger slides under. Then light vetwrap; label ON the wrap. WHY each: anchor / stabilize / strain-relief. EXPECT: hub immobile when you wiggle the T-port. COMMON ERROR: circumferential tension ("secure = tight") — the tourniquet that makes tomorrow's fat paw; or all three strips doing the same job badly. RECOVERY: any doubt about tightness — cut it off and re-tape now; tape is cheaper than a swollen limb. STOP: n/a.
**Step 9 — Post-wrap recheck + chart.** DO: flush AGAIN through the finished wrap (taping kinks catheters, especially at the feline carpus); chart date/time, limb, gauge, attempts, flush quality, tolerance, initials; case-log it. WHY: the wrap changed the geometry; prove it still works before you walk away. COMMON ERROR: skipping because "it flushed two minutes ago."

## 7. Tactile medicine
Threading DONE RIGHT: a smooth, faintly gritty glide — like sliding a coffee stirrer through a drinking straw. Threading WRONG: a springy, building push-back — the catheter tip crumpling against wall or valve; your fingers feel it buckle microscopically. LEARN BOTH ON THE MODEL until your finger refuses to push through the second one. The flush: a good line pushes like water through a straw; infiltration pushes like water into bread dough — a soft, absorbing wrongness; occlusion pushes like a capped syringe — hard wall. The three sensations are the whole diagnostic kit your thumb owns for the catheter's entire dwell life.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**No flash:** identical ladder to CSC-VEN-01 (§8) — with the addendum that catheter units flash slower; give it one full second at depth before declaring dry. **Flash, won't thread:** run the sequence — advance the unit 1 mm more, flatten more, quarter-turn WHILE threading; if resistance persists: valve, bifurcation, or extraluminal tip → unit out together, pressure, move proximal or other limb. NEVER the pull-back-re-thread (§2). **Threads but flush blebs:** the catheter entered and exited (through-and-through placement) or tip migrated on securing — remove, pressure, honest log entry, new site. **Threads, flushed fine, dies during taping:** kink at carpus (cats) or hub lever against a tape ridge — re-tape with the carpus in neutral; per-policy splint for serial offenders. **Vein blows on entry (immediate bloom):** back wall; done at this site; the distal-first strategy just paid for itself — go up. **Everything's failing and the patient needs access:** say the useful sentence early — "two attempts, both distal right, vein blows on entry; left untouched; she's getting harder to hold." Escalation with data is a skill deliverable, not defeat.
**Maintain-phase troubleshooting (both tracks own this):** pump occlusion alarm → trace-then-site, positional test (flex/extend the carpus gently — flow returns = positional; report for re-tape/splint decision); wet wrap → stop pump, unwrap per direction, inspect — leak at hub (loose T-port) vs displacement (out); cool doughy swelling = infiltration (stop, report, expect removal); warm red cord + flush-pain = phlebitis pattern (report, expect removal order); catheter fell out in the kennel → pressure the site, save the catheter FOR INSPECTION (is the tip complete?), report — a missing tip fragment is an escalation, not an oh-well.

## 9. Real patients
**Cats:** shorter catheters, carpus-kink planning, tape with the leg in a natural stance-angle; many cats tolerate placement better with the head towel-covered. **Puppies:** vein diameter fine, but zero impulse control — a second holder on the rear half changes everything; consider placing during a distraction feed. **Dehydrated/shocky:** flat veins + urgent need = the worst combination; pre-warm, use your best single attempt, and escalate fast — these are exactly the patients where senior hands should place early, and where a staged-and-ready table (your job) saves minutes that matter. **Geriatric fragile veins:** blow on contact; drop a gauge, flatten early, thread gently. **Chondrodystrophics (dachshunds, corgis):** two-centimeter runways — start at the carpal dorsum, expect the hub to sit near your entry point. **Obese:** the vein lives deeper than it palpates; commit to the flatten-advance beat on faith in your landmarks. **Previously catheterized (the rehospitalized chronic):** scarred, valve-y veins that flash and refuse threads — expect it, say it in your plan, budget attempts accordingly.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **The first-flash thread.** Consequence: sub-q catheter that "placed fine." Prevention: the flatten-advance beat said OUT LOUD. Every senior will drill this; internalize it before they have to. 2. **Pull-back-re-thread.** Consequence: possible shear = embolized plastic. Prevention: unit-out reflex; the model lab makes you practice failing correctly. 3. **Proximal-first siting.** Consequence: one blown attempt orphans the vein. 4. **Unprimed T-port.** Consequence: air push into a patient. Prevention: prime at staging, cap, done. 5. **Tape tourniquets.** Consequence: fat paw by morning. Prevention: one-finger test on every strip, every time. 6. **Stylet on the table.** Consequence: someone's hand finds it. 7. **Skipping the post-wrap flush.** Consequence: the night shift discovers your kink at 2 AM with a pressure alarm. 8. **Force-flushing "to open it up."** Consequence: clot shower or sub-q bolus. Prevention: the three-sensation thumb (§7) plus the never-force rule.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: tear tape BEFORE you glove/stick — one-handed tape-tearing over a fresh catheter is a circus act with a casualty. · Rest your catheter-hand's heel on the patient's leg; the unit rides movement instead of fighting it. · On slow flashes, watch the hub chamber against something white. · Write the label DURING staging; post-placement you'll have one hand busy forever. · For cats: tape job rehearsed mentally BEFORE the stick — the difference between a 90-second and a 5-minute secure is planning, and minute three is when cats leave. · The best "difficult catheter" tool in the building is a second pair of experienced hands EARLY — pride is not a gauge size. BANNED: re-threading over the stylet in any form, including "just a millimeter"; "testing patency" by cranking the flush; cutting tape off with the same scissors' tip pointed at the catheter (slide a finger under and cut over your own finger); leaving a questionable catheter in service "for the day shift to sort."

## 14. Technique variation & house standard
**Outcome requirements:** a catheter whose plastic sits in the lumen, flushes without resistance or bleb, is secured so a tug reaches the tape and not the vein, is labelled, and survives the shift.

**CVAH house standards** `[HOUSE]`: distal-first siting (reason: preserves the vein above for the retry); label every catheter with date/time/gauge/initials (reason: dwell-time surveillance for phlebitis); post-wrap re-flush (reason: taping kinks catheters, and the night shift inherits what you didn't check).

**Genuinely variable:** tape architecture — the three-strip pattern taught here is one good method among several that achieve anchor + stabilize + strain-relief; some experienced people here butterfly the first strip, others use a chevron; T-port versus injection cap; damming with thumb-over-tip versus hub pressure; one-handed versus two-handed threading.

## 15. What may vary without being wrong
> Do not fail a learner for: a different but sound tape pattern that passes the one-finger test and directs strain to tape · gauge choice one size either way when clinically reasonable and the order didn't specify · aspirating to confirm before flushing, or not · a quarter-turn while threading · removing a hesitant catheter early rather than working it — **early removal of a doubtful catheter is good judgment.**

## 16. Precision audit
`[EVIDENCE/mechanical]`: the 1–2 mm advance after flash reflects real catheter geometry — the plastic tip trails the steel — so it is not arbitrary scaffolding.
`[GUIDE]`: 15–25° entry · 1–3 mL flush volume · 90-second taping target · ≥10 model placements.
`[HOUSE]`: distal-first · labelling · post-wrap re-flush.
`[SAFETY]`: **never re-advance a catheter over its stylet** (shear and embolism — this absolute is load-bearing and stays) · stylet direct to sharps · never force a flush.
