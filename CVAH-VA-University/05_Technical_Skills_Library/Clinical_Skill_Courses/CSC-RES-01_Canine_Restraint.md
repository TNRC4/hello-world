# CSC-RES-01 — Canine Restraint
Companion to TS-RES-001/002/003 · Benchmark course · ~30 min + daily floor practice

## 1. Purpose
Restraint is the platform under every procedure in the building. A good restrainer makes a blood draw a one-minute non-event; a bad one turns a nail trim into a bite report and a dog who fights every visit for life. The skill is not strength — it's geometry, timing, and reading. The strongest person in the building is usually not the best restrainer; the calmest usually is.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
The escalation ladder (from CSC-level handling): lip-lick/yawn/look-away → whale eye/freeze/tucked tail → growl/air-snap → bite. Every rung you notice early is force you don't need later. Force physics: dogs push INTO steady pressure and explode AGAINST sudden pressure — a firm, boring, continuous hold beats a reactive clutching one. Anatomy that matters: the trachea is midline under the neck (your forearm never presses there); limbs lever at joints (hold ABOVE joints — grabbing a carpus gives the dog a lever and you a sprain); the head owns the bite arc — control the head, and everything else is management.

## 3. Equipment
Non-slip mat (the single most underrated restraint tool — a dog scrabbling on steel is a dog panicking); leash/slip lead; basket and sleeve muzzles in a size run; towels; treats within the holder's reach. Table at working height for small/medium dogs; floor for giants — never fight gravity plus a 60 kg dog.

## 4. Patient preparation
Read before touching: loose-wiggly (standard holds), frozen-staring (decompress first — this dog is closest to the bite), panting-pacing (short holds, breaks between tasks). Check the record for flags; honor them completely (CSC rule: a "will bite" flag is a plan requirement, not a challenge). Batch the visit's procedures so one good hold covers three tasks.

## 5–6. The holds — geometry, hands, and steps
### Standing restraint (temps, SQ injections, exams)
DO: stand at the dog's side; one arm UNDER the neck, hand cupping around so the head is snugged against your shoulder/chest, muzzle pointed AWAY from the proceduralist; other arm under the belly just in front of the stifles, forearm lifting slightly so sitting is pre-empted; your body leans gently along the dog's side. WHY: the under-neck arm owns the bite arc; the belly arm owns the sit; your torso owns the sideways bail. EXPECT: the dog settles into the lean within a few seconds; you feel breathing rhythm through your side — that contact IS your monitor. COMMON ERROR: elbow winging out so the neck arm presses the throat; or holding the head so loosely it can whip 180°. RECOVERY: reset grip during a calm beat, not mid-struggle. STOP: escalating panic; any airway sound; anyone's stop-call.
### Sternal restraint (cephalic draws, exams, most everything)
DO: dog sitting or sternal; same under-neck hug; second arm over the shoulders, hand tucking the far forelimb or presenting the near one to the phlebotomist; lean in. For draws, YOUR hand behind the elbow pushes the limb forward — push the elbow, never pull the paw. EXPECT: a stable tripod of dog + your two arms + your chest. COMMON ERROR: face directly over the dog's skull in the bite arc; drifting attention (chatting) while your hands loosen in sync with your focus. STOP: as above.
### Lateral restraint (rads, saphenous, wounds)
DO (the takedown): from standing at the side, reach OVER the back and grasp the far-side forelimb above the carpus and far-side hindlimb above the hock; lift the far limbs toward you as your body guides the dog's slide DOWN YOUR LEGS to the mat — controlled the whole way, never a drop. Then: forearm across the neck (weight on the shoulder end, nothing on the trachea) with that hand holding the DOWN-side forelimb above the carpus; other forearm across the hindquarters, hand on down-side hindlimb. WHY: holding down-side limbs prevents the push-off that rights a dog; your forearms do the holding so your hands stay free-ish and soft. EXPECT: brief paddle then stillness; narrate calmly throughout. COMMON ERROR: grabbing at joints; releasing the hindquarters to help the front (the rear is where escapes start); pressing the neck harder when the front paddles (press the SHOULDER, reassure, wait). RECOVERY: if the dog is winning, don't crank — call it, let the dog stand, replan. STOP: cyanosis/gasp (release position immediately), escalating panic, stop-call. Release protocol: announce "releasing," ease pressure rear-first, prevent the leap off the table.
### Venipuncture holder role (cephalic/jugular/saphenous)
Covered in CSC-VEN-01/02/03 from the sticker's side; your deltas as holder: your occluding thumb takes cues ("occlude" / "release") instantly; you are ALSO the patient monitor — face, gums, breathing — because the sticker's eyes are on the vein. The holder owns the stop-call more than anyone.

## 7. Tactile medicine
Correct restraint pressure feels like a firm hug — the dog can breathe, shift weight, and lean, but not reposition its skeleton. You'll feel escalation before you see it: muscles board-up, breathing shortens, the lean disappears (the dog taking its weight back is the wind-up). A dog going still-and-loose is settling; a dog going still-and-hard is loading. The difference lives in your forearms — train yourself to narrate it ("he's gone tight").

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**The dog beats the takedown:** don't wrestle mid-air; let it stand, reset the mat and your grip, try once more with a second person on the rear half. Two failed takedowns = different plan (standing rads? sedation conversation?). **Struggling inside a hold:** first check for a pain source (are YOU on a sore hip?), soften 10% for two seconds (paradox: many dogs settle when the squeeze relents), reposition ONE limb at a time. Continuous struggle >15–20 s = stop; nobody wins extended wrestling. **The muzzle moment:** indications = flags, growl history, painful procedure on a worried dog. Application: calm, from the side, treat before and after; basket for anything longer than a minute (they can pant); NEVER on vomiting/dyspneic patients. A dog that fights the muzzle itself = plan change, not a second wrestler. **Escapes:** prevention is door-audits; if loose in the room — do not grab at the body (bite risk); block, lower your posture, leash-lasso calmly. Loose in a corridor = call it loudly ("LOOSE DOG, closing doors") — pride costs seconds. **Giant breeds:** floor work, two handlers as default, your body weight leaning along the spine does what arms can't. **When to hand off:** any bite attempt; any hold requiring more force than a firm hug; your own fear (dogs read it — a scared holder is an unsafe holder; saying so is professionalism).

## 9. Real patients
**Puppies:** wiggle without malice — contain, don't clamp; make every visit-end sweet (you are building or wrecking a lifetime of vet visits). **Geriatric/arthritic:** ask the record which joints hurt; pad the mat; lateral via a supported ease-down, not the standard slide. **Brachycephalics:** NO neck-hug compression, sternal default, watch for the purple-tongue moment, breaks between tasks. **Deep-chested giants:** lateral on these is a two-person minimum; consider standing alternatives for rads per DVM. **Painful patients:** analgesia conversation BEFORE restraint (advocate: "can she have her pain meds before we position?"); expect bite risk regardless of temperament. **The frozen-fearful:** slowest hands, lowest voice, treats raining; consider whether today's list can shrink (ask the DVM — "he's at his limit; which items are essential?" is high-value advocacy). **Sedated patients:** never trust sedation to substitute for support — a sedated dog can still bite and can definitely fall; positioning duties shift toward airway/comfort guarding.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Strength-first mindset** — clamping harder as the answer to everything; consequence: escalation cycles; prevention: geometry-and-timing coaching, watching the calm seniors. 2. **Trachea-forearm** — pressing the airway during lateral; drilled out with the shoulder-press cue. 3. **Joint-grabbing** — carpi and hocks as handles; injury to dog and holder. 4. **Face in the bite arc** while leaning over the skull. 5. **Attention drift** — hands loosen exactly when the room chats; the incident always happens "suddenly." 6. **Springing releases** — letting go all at once; dogs launch; announce and ease. 7. **Taking struggle personally** — cranking down on a dog "being bad"; fear isn't disobedience; the fix is the plan, not the pressure.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: The mat first, always — traction is tranquility. · Narrate transitions ("lifting now") — teams and dogs both startle less. · Feed the floor: a lick-mat smeared with something during standing holds converts half of wiggly dogs to statues. · Your exhale is contagious — audible slow breathing settles the dog under your arm (and you). · Handler height beats handler strength: get your center of gravity over the dog's. BANNED: alpha-roll/dominance framing (fear escalates fear; it also gets people bitten); suspending by scruff or limbs; "hugging tighter until it stops" as a doctrine; muzzling to skip the reading ("muzzle it and go" produces the next bite at muzzle-off).
