# CSC-SUR-01 — Surgical Patient Preparation (Clip & Scrub)
Companion to TS-SUR-PREP-001 · Benchmark course · ~30 min + coached cases

## 1. Purpose
The prep creates the sterile field's foundation on living skin. Every surgical-site infection starts as a lapse somebody didn't notice — a stingy clip, a scrub that went center-ward, a hot blade, a wrong limb. Your prep also runs on the anesthesia clock: every prep minute is an anesthetized minute. The goal is a prep that is CORRECT first and fast second, and gets faster only by eliminating wasted motion, never by shaving corners.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
Bacteria live ON skin (transient organisms your scrub removes) and IN it (resident flora in follicles you can only suppress — why the field also gets draped and why "sterile skin" doesn't exist, only surgically clean). Contamination physics from W5-02: touch, droplet, hover-rain, and moisture wicking (strike-through). The scrub's two active ingredients are CHEMISTRY (agent) and TIME×FRICTION (your hands) — cutting either halves the product. Hair is contamination: it cannot be sterilized, only removed, and it travels; that's why clipping happens in prep, never in the OR.

## 3. Equipment
Clippers with a SHARP #40 blade (a dull blade doesn't cut hair — it drags and burns skin; blade sharpness is a patient-safety item, not a maintenance nicety); blade coolant/spare blades; vacuum; chlorhexidine scrub + solution/alcohol per CVAH protocol `[CVAH-SPECIFIC INPUT REQUIRED: agents, cycle counts, contact times — post on prep-bay card]`; gauze stacks; non-sterile gloves; positioning aids (troughs, ties, sandbags); warming already active under the patient (small patients chill during prep, not just surgery); eye lube confirmed with the anesthetist.

## 4. Patient preparation
The patient arrives anesthetized: from this moment its airway, depth, and welfare belong to the anesthetist — but its POSITION and SKIN belong to you. Confirm before a single hair falls: patient ID, procedure, SITE AND SIDE — read the consent/record yourself AND verbalize to the anesthetist ("Bella Chen, left stifle — confirm?"). A board-vs-record mismatch is a full stop for the whole room, and being the one who catches it is a badge, not an annoyance.

## 5. Operator preparation
You stand on the side that gives your dominant hand a natural clipper stroke along the site; vacuum within reach; scrub bowls arranged in use-order AWAY from the patient's feet (kick zone). Non-dominant hand's permanent job: skin management — stretching it taut ahead of the clipper, tensioning scrub gauze zones. Plan your margins BEFORE clipping (procedure card): visualize the incision, then the drape window, then add the retraction-and-extension margin — clip THAT.

## 6. The procedure
**Step 1 — Site/side liturgy.** DO: verbalized confirmation as above; check the record, not your memory of morning rounds. STOP: any mismatch = nothing proceeds until the DVM resolves it. This is the step with the never-forgiven failure mode; treat it with liturgical seriousness every single time.
**Step 2 — Clip.** DO: taut skin ahead of the blade (your other hand walking just in front); blade FLAT to the skin — the edge digs, the flat glides; first pass WITH the coat's grain (debulks), second AGAINST (closeness); margins per your visualized window, generous always; check blade heat against your inner wrist every 60–90 s — hot = swap/cool. WHY: flat-and-taut is the entire burn-and-nick prevention system. EXPECT: smooth naked skin, no red drag lines, no "corduroy" stripes (dull blade sign). COMMON ERROR: tenting loose skin into the blade (nick), digging the edge at angle changes (burn stripe), skimpy margins that the draping team discovers too late. RECOVERY: ANY nick or burn: report to the surgeon before draping — they decide; concealment converts an incident into an offense. STOP: skin lesions discovered under the coat (dermatitis, wounds at the site) = pause, show the DVM before proceeding.
**Step 3 — Vacuum + degrease.** DO: vacuum the field AND the surrounding table/patient (loose hair migrates); a first degreasing wipe if house protocol uses one. EXPECT: a field where a white gauze wiped across comes away hairless.
**Step 4 — The pattern scrub.** DO: gauze with scrub agent, start ON the incision line, scrub with friction in a spiral working OUTWARD; at the margin, the gauze is DONE — trash, new gauze, start at center again. Repeat cycles per protocol, interleaving alcohol/solution passes as specified; endpoint = BOTH the minimum cycle count AND visibly clean gauze, whichever comes later. WHY: the gradient rule — center is cleanest, margins dirtiest; any inward stroke re-imports what you exported. EXPECT: methodical rhythm, ~30–60 s per cycle; the field develops the uniform "surgical tan" of chlorhex. COMMON ERROR: the absent-minded back-track (one inward wipe undoes the cycle — restart the cycle, and say so; honesty about your own contamination is trained here because it's the same muscle the OR runs on); drenching (pooling under the patient = chemical burn + hypothermia + strike-through highway — use damp, not dripping, gauze and towel dams on dependent edges). STOP: n/a.
**Step 5 — Peripheral duties while scrubbing.** DO: monitor position stability (a slipping trough mid-scrub = re-position AND re-scrub disturbed zones); guard warmth (blankets over non-field areas); communicate timeline to the room ("two cycles to go"). WHY: you are the room's only person watching the patient's non-field body right now.
**Step 6 — Transport & handover.** DO: move to the OR with the field UP and untouched — nothing brushes it: no arms, no leads, no enthusiastic blankets; position on the OR table per surgeon's setup; final sterile prep happens in-room per protocol (may be CVT). Announce: "prepped and untouched." COMMON ERROR: the doorway shoulder-brush; leads dragged across the field during hookup. RECOVERY: anything touches the prepped field = say it immediately — a re-prep in prep-bay minutes is cheap; a contaminated incision is not.

## 7. Tactile medicine
Clipper feel: a sharp blade glides with a low hum and hair falls away; a dull one CHATTERS, drags, and needs pressure — pressure is your warning sign, not your solution. Blade heat check: touch the blade flat to your inner wrist — comfortable-warm is fine; "coffee mug fresh from the microwave" means stop. Taut skin under your spreading fingers feels drum-like; loose folds feel like they'll fold — and folds are where nicks happen. Scrub friction: think "cleaning a pan you like" — real pressure, small circles, not surface petting.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**Clipper trail looks striped/ragged:** dull blade — swap it; re-clip strays; log the blade for sharpening (the next prep inherits your diligence or your neglect). **Nicked the skin:** pressure, report, surgeon decides (site shift? still usable?); document. **Scrub interrupted mid-cycle** (anesthetist needs hands, patient moves): note where you were; disturbed or paused-too-long fields get the cycle restarted — cycles are integrity units, not suggestions. **Patient too light — moving during prep:** STOP clipping/scrubbing near anything delicate, tell the anesthetist, wait; a moving patient plus a blade is your call to pause, always. **Site/side doubt creeps in mid-prep** ("did they say left?"): stop and re-verify — mid-prep doubt outranks momentum, every time. **You realize your margins are too small at draping:** own it fast — extending clip+scrub NOW under the surgeon's direction beats a cramped field mid-procedure. **Pooling discovered under the patient:** dry it, dam it, note skin condition post-op (dependent chlorhex burns show later — the report protects the patient AND you).

## 9. Real patients
**Cats/small dogs:** hypothermia is the co-procedure — warm scrub per protocol, minimal wetting, speed matters more here (organized speed, not hurried corners). **Deep-coated breeds:** double-length clip time — budget it in the schedule voice ("this coat needs ten extra minutes") rather than eating it from scrub cycles. **Thin-skinned seniors/sighthounds:** tissue-paper skin nicks on a glance — flattest blade angle, slowest strokes, maximum taut. **Brachycephalics:** prep positioning must respect the airway — never let positioning aids crowd the neck; the anesthetist confirms every position change. **Emergency preps:** the DVM may direct an abbreviated protocol — that's THEIR call to make out loud, never your silent shortcut; your job is executing the stated abbreviation cleanly and saying what was and wasn't done. **Contaminated sites (abscess, degloving):** these run their own DVM-directed sequence (often clean-surrounding-first); ask before defaulting to the routine pattern.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Margin optimism** — clipping to the incision, not the drape window. 2. **Blade-heat obliviousness** — the burn discovered at suture removal. 3. **The inward wipe** — muscle memory from cleaning tables; drilled out with the trash-at-the-margin rule. 4. **Drenching** — more agent misread as more asepsis. 5. **Silent site doubt** — proceeding around a mismatch feeling. 6. **Field-brush at the doorway** — transport casualness. 7. **Prep-bay tunnel vision** — losing the patient (temp, position, anesthetist's needs) to the site.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: clip the margins FIRST, then fill in — the outline commits you to correct size before fatigue argues for less. · Count your scrub cycles out loud softly; interruptions can't steal your count. · Keep one dry towel corner under your non-dominant hand for instant pooling response. · A gauze square held at the field edge catches clipper-check hair before it lands. · Learn each surgeon's margin preferences ONCE and write them on the procedure cards — asked-and-recorded beats guessed-twice. BANNED: "pretty clean" as a scrub endpoint; alcohol-only speed-preps on your own initiative; blade-dip cooling in scrub solution (contaminates and doesn't cool); re-using dropped gauze because "it landed clean-side up."
