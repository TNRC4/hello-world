# CSC-VEN-01 — Canine Cephalic Venipuncture
Companion to TS-VEN-CEPH-001 (governance) · Benchmark exemplar course · ~35 min + lab

## 1. Purpose
You are collecting diagnostic blood from the vein on top of the dog's forearm. It's the workhorse draw: convenient position, patient facing forward, holder and sticker both comfortable. Every CBC, chemistry, and heartworm test starts here. Done well, it takes ninety seconds and the patient barely notices; done badly, it produces bruised legs, ruined samples the DVM makes decisions on anyway, patients who fight the next visit, and a technician who has to re-do your work plus their own.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge (the five facts that make your hands smart)
1. The cephalic vein runs up the TOP of the forearm, slightly to the inside — from just above the paw toward the elbow crook. It is superficial: just under the skin, not buried in muscle.
2. It is a vein, so blood flows TOWARD the heart (paw→elbow). Your holder blocks that flow at the elbow, the vein backs up and fattens. No occlusion, no vein.
3. Vein walls are soft. Suction stronger than the vein's refill rate sucks the walls shut — and shears red cells (hemolysis). Gentle wins.
4. Clotting starts the moment blood leaves the vessel. The trip from syringe to (correctly ordered) tubes is a race measured in seconds.
5. Attempted sticks damage the vessel you'll need next time. The vein is a shared, exhaustible resource — treat every stick as spending it.

## 3. Equipment (and why)
- **Needle:** 22 g for most dogs (green in most brands — confirm house stock). 25 g for toy breeds/puppies (slower, gentler; expect a slow draw). Bigger needle ≠ better: 20 g in a small vein tears more than it collects.
- **Syringe:** just big enough for the ordered volume — 3 mL for most panels. A 12 mL syringe on a 22 g needle generates collapse-grade suction with a light thumb; oversizing the syringe is oversizing your worst habit.
- **Butterfly (winged) set 23–25 g:** your friend for wigglers, toys, and fragile veins — the flexible line means paw movement doesn't lever the needle out of the vessel.
- **Tubes** in fill order, standing in a rack ON the table before you start. Gauze squares ×3. Alcohol. Sharps container within arm's reach — not across the room. Pen for patient-side labeling.

## 4. Patient preparation
Quick pre-read: is this dog loose and wiggly (needs firm holder), frozen and staring (needs decompression, slow approach), or panting/distressed (does this draw need to happen right now this way — ask). Check BOTH forelimbs for existing catheters, catheter-planned flags (surgery today?), bruises from previous attempts, wounds. If the right leg has yesterday's hematoma, start left. Position: sitting or sternal on a non-slip mat, at a table height where the extended forearm is at YOUR mid-torso — you should not be stooping or shrugging.

## 5. Operator preparation — where everybody goes
- **Holder** stands at the dog's side, arm around the neck in the hug hold, other hand behind the elbow of the draw leg: pushing it forward from BEHIND the joint (never pulling the paw — dogs retract from paw-pulls), thumb rolling across the elbow crook to occlude.
- **You** kneel or sit directly IN FRONT of the extended limb, eyes over the forearm. Your NON-dominant hand owns the limb: fingers wrapped under the forearm, thumb alongside the vein below your entry point, pulling the skin gently down toward the paw (distal traction). Your dominant hand owns the needle, bevel up, held like a dart — pads of thumb and index on the hub, not choked up on the barrel.
- **Line of sight:** you're looking down the barrel of the forearm. The syringe sits in line with the vein's long axis, not crossing it.
- **Anticipate:** dogs pull the leg BACK and DOWN. Your traction hand is also your early-warning system — you'll feel the retraction start a half-second before it shows.

## 6. The procedure
**Step 1 — Verify, out loud.** DO: "This is Bella Chen, cephalic draw for CBC and chem?" while checking two identifiers. WHY: wrong-patient is the unforgivable error class. EXPECT: holder confirms. COMMON ERROR: mumbling it as ritual while reading the room instead of the band. RECOVERY: n/a — this one you just do. STOP: any mismatch, full stop.

**Step 2 — Stage everything.** DO: needle seated on syringe, plunger broken loose (one pull-push in the wrapper), tubes racked in fill order, gauze open, sharps lid open. WHY: a staged table means the needle is in the vein for seconds, not minutes; an unstaged table means blood clotting in your syringe while you hunt for the lavender top. EXPECT: you can reach everything without looking. COMMON ERROR: starting the stick, THEN asking someone to grab tubes. RECOVERY: if you catch yourself mid-stick without tubes — finish the draw calmly, hand the syringe off for immediate transfer, restage before your next patient. STOP: n/a.

**Step 3 — Raise the vein.** DO: cue the holder ("occlude, please"), watch the crook of the elbow, then LOOK AWAY from the leg and palpate: run your index fingertip across the forearm's top surface. WHY: the vein is found by feel — a soft, springy, slightly warm cord that refills under a light press. Sight lies (fur, pigment, shadow); touch doesn't. EXPECT: 3–8 seconds for filling; the vein rises like a slow breath. In thick-coated dogs you'll feel it long before any chance of seeing it. COMMON ERROR: impatience — declaring "no vein" at second two; or pressing so hard while palpating that you occlude the very thing you're finding. RECOVERY: release-and-reocclude if you've hunted >60–90 s (trapped blood hemoconcentrates and the vein stops refilling crisply). STOP: if nothing raises on either limb after honest attempts — flat veins on a sick patient are information; tell the CVT.

**Step 4 — Fix the vein in place.** DO: with the vein mapped, set your thumb parallel to it, one to two finger-widths BELOW your entry site, and draw the skin snug toward the paw. WHY: the cephalic's failure mode is rolling — it slides sideways off the needle point. Taut skin pins it. EXPECT: the vein feels slightly less mobile under your fingertip; skin has no wrinkle slack. COMMON ERROR: thumb ON TOP of the vein (you'll stick your own thumbnail's shadow), or traction so light it decorates rather than restrains. RECOVERY: reset your grip — never chase a rolling vein with needle angle alone. STOP: n/a.

**Step 5 — Enter.** DO: alcohol swab (parts the fur — now you may even see it), bevel up, 15–30° to the skin, aim along the vein's line, one deliberate motion through skin and into the vessel. In thick-skinned or scarred dogs, two-stage is fine: through skin beside the vein, small pause, then into the vein. WHY: slow needle passage hurts more and gives the vein time to roll; steep angles go through the back wall. EXPECT: skin gives a brief resistance then releases; vein entry in many dogs is a subtle "pop" — softer than the skin. Watch the hub. COMMON ERROR: the flinch-stick — jabbing fast and shallow from fear of hurting the dog, ending up on top of, beside, or through the vein. RECOVERY: if you feel you're skating on the vein's surface, drop your angle 5–10° and advance with the traction hand snugging harder. STOP: patient explodes into struggle — needle out, regroup with the holder before anything else.

**Step 6 — Flash, flatten, seat.** DO: at first blood in the hub, drop the syringe nearly parallel to the leg and advance 1–2 mm. WHY: the flash means the bevel's TIP is in; the whole bevel isn't. Aspirating now sucks wall onto the opening. EXPECT: the syringe now lies almost along the limb; the needle feels anchored, not tippy. COMMON ERROR: celebrating the flash by pulling the plunger instantly — flow for half a second, then nothing. RECOVERY: that IS the flash-then-stop below; flatten and re-seat first. STOP: n/a.

**Step 7 — Aspirate like you're sipping.** DO: pull the plunger with about the force you'd use to lift a coin — a few millimeters at a time; let the barrel fill at the vein's pace. Small dog = slow fill; that's physiologic, not failure. WHY: your thumb sets the pressure differential; the vein's refill sets the speed limit. Exceed it and the walls clap shut. EXPECT: steady dark red climb; slight pulsing with the traction hand's grip is normal. COMMON ERROR: ratcheting harder as flow slows — the death spiral (harder pull → tighter collapse → harder pull → hemolyzed half-sample). RECOVERY: ease OFF the plunger, let the vein re-expand two seconds, resume gentler. STOP: patient stress escalating (see the holder's face, not just the leg) — a two-thirds sample and a calm patient beats a full tube and a fight.

**Step 8 — Exit in the order that prevents hematoma.** DO: say "release" (holder's thumb OFF, restraint stays ON) → withdraw the needle smoothly along its own line → holder or you clamps gauze on the site with real pressure. WHY: pulling a needle out of a still-pressurized vein pushes blood into the tissue — that's the hematoma you'll be apologized for tomorrow. EXPECT: a clean site; a single drop at most. COMMON ERROR: the order scramble under time pressure — needle out first, THEN "release." RECOVERY: firm pressure 60+ s, say what happened, check before kennel return. STOP: n/a.

**Step 9 — The second race.** DO: needle OFF the syringe (or transfer device per house policy), blood run down the tube WALL: citrate to its line first if coags ordered, then lavender — filled and inverted 8–10 times immediately — then the rest. Gentle wrist rolls, never shakes. Label every tube at the table, before your hand leaves it. WHY: everything in TS-SAM-001; the summary is *anticoagulant tubes are on the clock and stoppers punched by needles eat cells.* EXPECT: 20–30 unhurried seconds. COMMON ERROR: carrying naked tubes to the bench "to label there." RECOVERY: if interrupted mid-transfer, the person who drew finishes the transfer — handoffs mid-race cause both clots and swaps. STOP: n/a.

**Step 10 — Close the loop.** DO: needle to sharps as one motion; site recheck (lift the gauze — dry? no growing softness?); patient decompressed and praised; chart: site/side, gauge, attempts, complications-or-none, tolerance, initials; case-log entry. WHY: the site recheck catches the slow hematoma; the chart is where your work becomes trustworthy. COMMON ERROR: charting three draws from memory at lunch. STOP: n/a.

## 7. Tactile medicine — what your fingers will learn
A GOOD raised vein: a cooked-spaghetti cord with spring — press lightly, it dents and refills. A FLAT vein: a cord with no spring, like the spaghetti uncooked (dehydration, fear-vasoconstriction, or your holder's occlusion is soft). ROLLING: under your palpating finger the cord SLIDES laterally a few millimeters — that slide-distance is exactly what your distal traction must remove. SKIN ENTRY: brief firm resistance, then give; old dogs' skin is tissue paper (almost no resistance — easy to overshoot), young mastiffs' is leather (real push needed — brace your traction hand so the "give" doesn't become a lunge). VEIN ENTRY: a softer, subtler give — many people feel it as the needle suddenly "floating." THROUGH THE BACK WALL: a second give you did NOT want; flash stops; often a fine grating as the bevel touches deeper tissue. COLLAPSE ON ASPIRATION: the plunger goes rubbery-hard mid-pull, like sucking a thick shake through a bent straw — that's your cue to ease off, not pull through.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**No flash at expected depth.** See: clean entry, dry hub. Likely: beside the vein (rolled — commonest), over it, or too shallow. Check first: your traction (did the skin wrinkle when you entered?), then palpate with your free hand WITHOUT fully exiting — locate the cord relative to your needle. Correct: withdraw to just under the skin, re-fix the vein, redirect along the corrected line — that's still the SAME attempt. Don't: sweep the needle side-to-side underground hunting ("windshield-wiping" lacerates tissue and vessel). After one honest redirect fails: exit, pressure, breathe, re-palpate from scratch — that was attempt one.
**Flash, then nothing.** See: blood entered the hub then stopped. Feel: plunger rubbery (collapse) vs simply blank (bevel against wall / half-out). Do in order: flatten the angle → advance 1 mm → rotate the syringe a quarter-turn (walks the bevel off the wall) → ease aspiration pressure → if still dry, have holder release-and-reocclude (refills the vein). Two cycles of that with nothing = treat as a failed attempt; exit with the correct release order.
**Blood slows mid-draw.** Same ladder as above, plus: check the patient — position shifted? limb rotated in the holder's grip? A returning lean or twist kinks the vessel upstream. Fix the patient's geometry before blaming the needle.
**Hematoma ballooning DURING the draw.** You're through the back wall or the entry is leaking around the needle. Stop the draw — needle out (correct order), firm pressure 90+ s. That limb's site is spent; your next move is the other limb or a saphenous, and your attempt count is at one-spent.
**Patient escalating.** The holder calling it, open-mouth breathing, screaming disproportionate to the stick, thrashing your traction hand can't damp: STOP. Not "after this tube." The decision tree after calm returns: different site with towel/second-holder plan, CVT takeover, or DVM sedation conversation. You get zero style points for finishing a draw on a panicking patient and you lose the NEXT six visits' cooperation.
**When someone else takes over:** after YOUR two attempts, or sooner whenever your confidence breaks — say the useful sentence: "Two attempts, right cephalic, vein rolls hard and I lost flash both times; left leg untouched." That sentence is competence, not failure.

## 9. Real patients
- **Puppies/toys:** vein like wet thread. 25 g + tiny syringe or butterfly, expect 60+ seconds of slow fill; warm the limb in your hand first; every mL matters — confirm the ordered volume is proportionate (flag >1% of body weight in mL to the CVT).
- **Geriatrics:** fragile walls, tissue-paper skin, rolling veins in loose fascia — maximum traction, minimum suction, longer post-draw pressure (their bruises bloom).
- **Dehydrated/sick:** flat, slow-refilling veins. Butterfly, warmth, patience, and a lowered threshold to escalate — the patient whose veins are flat is the patient whose results are urgent.
- **Obese:** the vein sits deeper in a fat sleeve and doesn't show; palpation is everything, entry angle a touch steeper, then flatten as usual.
- **Thick-coated breeds:** don't fight the coat — clip a small window (owner-consent awareness for show dogs; ask first through the CSR).
- **The frightened-frozen dog:** stillness ≠ consent. Watch for the whale eye and bitten-off breath; use lateral-thinking (treats, floor draw for giant breeds, saphenous while "just petting").
- **Previously-bruised limbs:** yesterday's hematoma is today's no-go zone — draw above it or elsewhere; through a bruise hurts and samples poorly.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Visual hunting** — staring for a blue line instead of palpating. Why: eyes are how humans default. Consequence: misses in every thick-coated or dark-skinned patient. Prevention: the look-away palpation drill (Step 3). Recognizable: your gaze glued to the leg while your fingertip idles. Correctable: instantly, forever, once the finger learns.
2. **The instant-thread… instant-pull:** aspirating on first flash. Consequence: flash-then-stop on a textbook vein. Prevention: the "flatten-advance-THEN-pull" chant.
3. **Suction panic:** pulling harder as flow slows. Consequence: collapse + hemolysis + a redraw. Prevention: the coin-lift thumb image; a smaller syringe.
4. **Order scramble at exit:** needle out before occlusion released. Consequence: hematoma. Prevention: say "release" out loud every time — the verbal cue sequences your own hands.
5. **The naked-tube walk:** labeling at the bench. Consequence: the swap that ends careers. Prevention: pen lives in your draw kit.
6. **Windshield-wiping underground.** Consequence: tissue trauma, patient sensitization, still no blood. Prevention: withdraw-to-skin before every redirect.
7. **Third-stick fever:** "I almost had it." Consequence: spent veins, spent trust. Prevention: the two-attempt rule is not advice.

## 11. Experienced-staff pearls (and banned folklore)
PEARLS: Warm limb = fat vein — 30 s of your cupped hand beats 3 minutes of hunting on a cold morning. · Watch the accessory cephalic Y in bully breeds — the junction is often the fattest, most fixed target. · Set your tube rack to your non-dominant side so the transfer never crosses the sharps line. · On wigglers, rest the heel of your needle hand against the dog's leg — your hand rides limb movement instead of fighting it (works beautifully with a butterfly). · Tell the dog what's happening in a bored voice; bored voices lower everyone's pulse, including yours.
BANNED FOLKLORE: flicking/slapping the vein to "raise it" (it raises bruises); alcohol-drenching to see better (stings broken skin, cools the limb, dilutes samples); "quick recap just to carry it to sharps" (no); drawing from a hematoma "since it's blood anyway" (degraded, clotted, useless).

## 14. Technique variation & house standard
**Outcome requirements** (these are the job): a usable, correctly-filled, correctly-labelled sample; a patient no more stressed than when you started; a site that isn't bruising; no sharps incident.

**CVAH house standards** `[HOUSE]` — and why each exists:
- **Label at patient-side, before the tube leaves your hand.** Reason: swap prevention.
- **Two attempts, then hand off.** Reason: vein preservation and patient trust — and it removes the social pressure to keep trying. Counted per person.
- **Release occlusion before withdrawing.** Reason: hematoma prevention. The physiology is real, though the cost of getting it wrong is a bruise, not a catastrophe.

**What experienced people here genuinely do differently, and it's fine:** thumb anchored below the vein versus a two-finger stretch around it; entering beside the vein and walking into it versus straight in; butterfly by default versus needle-and-syringe by default; constant talking versus working in silence; alcohol first versus palpate-then-alcohol.

## 15. What may vary without being wrong
> An assessor **must not** fail a learner for any of these: entry angle anywhere in a shallow range that works for that limb · anchoring style (thumb beside, two-finger stretch, skin-pinch-and-roll) · needle-and-syringe versus butterfly · which hand takes the traction, if holder and phlebotomist have agreed · clipping a window versus parting fur with alcohol · counting a subcutaneous redirect as the same attempt or exiting and calling it two (the conservative reading is never wrong) · the order of gauze-versus-sharps in the final seconds, provided both happen and the sharp is never set down.

## 16. Precision audit
`[GUIDE]` scaffolding, vary freely: 15–30° entry · 1–2 mm advance after flash · 3–8 second fill wait · the "lift a coin" aspiration force · the cooked/uncooked-spaghetti analogy · 30–60 second pressure hold · ≥10 model sticks before live.
`[HOUSE]`: two-attempt limit · patient-side labelling · release-before-withdraw.
`[SAFETY]`: no hand recapping · sharps container within reach before uncapping · stop on patient distress · two-identifier check.
`[EVIDENCE]`: gentle aspiration to limit haemolysis; prompt anticoagulant mixing.
