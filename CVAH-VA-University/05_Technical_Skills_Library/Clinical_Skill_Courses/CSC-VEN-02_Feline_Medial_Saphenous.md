# CSC-VEN-02 — Feline Medial Saphenous Venipuncture
Companion to TS-VEN-SAPH-001 · Benchmark course · ~25 min + lab · Steps 1–2 and 8–10 (verify, stage, exit order, transfer, close-out) are identical to CSC-VEN-01 and are not repeated — master that course first.

## 1. Purpose
The medial saphenous is the cat vessel. It's large, flat, visible through sparse inner-thigh fur, and — the real reason — reachable while the cat stays wrapped in a towel with its head hidden and its dignity intact. A team fluent in this draw stops wrestling cats at the neck and forelimbs, which means calmer cats, better samples, and fewer shredded sleeves. It also spares forelimb veins for catheters.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
The vein runs along the INNER surface of the hindlimb — from the hock up the medial tibia toward the stifle and on toward the groin, superficial the whole way. Occlusion happens ABOVE it: pressure flat in the inguinal region (where thigh meets body wall). Unlike the cephalic it barely rolls — it's fixed against the tibia — but its wall is thin: it blows (hematomas) more easily, and it spasms smaller when the cat is stressed. Cats also decompensate under prolonged restraint faster than dogs: this whole draw is designed around a 60–90 second window of feline patience.

## 3. Equipment
25 g needle (23 g in big relaxed cats) on a 1–3 mL syringe — never larger; this vein punishes suction. Butterfly 25 g is superb here: the cat can twitch its leg without the needle levering. Towel (the restraint IS equipment). Tubes racked, gauze, alcohol, sharps in reach, pen.

## 4. Patient preparation
Read the cat BEFORE unwrapping anything: loose and blinking (proceed), frozen with dilated pupils (slow everything down, dim the room, consider whether this draw must happen now), panting or open-mouth (STOP — this cat sees the DVM before any needle). Check the record for prior "difficult" flags and plan accordingly (second person, cat muzzle-as-blindfold per policy, DVM sedation conversation). Position: lateral recumbency inside a loose burrito — body wrapped, BOTH hindlimbs out of the wrap's caudal end, head covered or shielded per the cat's preference. Most cats settle dramatically once the head is hidden.

## 5. Operator preparation
- **Holder** stands/sits at the cat's dorsal side: one forearm resting the wrapped body (weight of a hand, not a press), that hand's fingers curled over the upper hindlimb holding it out of your field; the OTHER hand applies inguinal occlusion on the draw leg — flat fingers pressed into the crease where the inner thigh meets the belly wall. Cue them: "flat and firm, like pressing a sponge, not pinching."
- **You** sit facing the cat's underside, the draw leg presented to you. Non-dominant hand cups the hock/lower limb, extending the leg gently and applying a whisper of distal traction — this vein needs stabilizing, not pinning. Dominant hand: needle bevel-up, aligned with the vein, angle LOW — 10–20°; this vessel is right under the skin.
- **Anticipate:** the feline escape move here is a leg-pump (piston kick) and a body-roll. Your cupping hand rides the leg; the holder's forearm damps the roll. If the cat starts a sustained struggle, that's not a grip problem to solve with force — it's a stop.

## 6. The procedure (unique steps)
**Raise.** DO: cue inguinal occlusion; watch the medial tibia. WHY/EXPECT: this vein appears visibly in most cats within 3–5 s — a soft blue-grey line; palpate to confirm spring. COMMON ERROR: holder pinching a fold of skin instead of pressing flat — no occlusion, just an annoyed cat. RECOVERY: re-cue "flat hand, press toward the body." STOP: leg thrashing that two gentle hands can't quiet.
**Enter.** DO: alcohol lightly (cold + wet annoys cats — one pass), 10–20° angle, one smooth motion; the vein is SHALLOW — you're aiming just under the skin, not into the leg. EXPECT: skin give is small; flash often instant. COMMON ERROR: dog-depth entry — straight through the vein into the tibialis; the "double give" here means you're done at this site. RECOVERY: through-and-through = exit, pressure 90 s, move UP the leg or to the other leg; the wall is too thin to fish back into. STOP: per stop criteria.
**Aspirate.** DO: coin-lift pressure, even lighter than the canine cephalic; watch for the barrel filling in slow pulses. EXPECT: 1–2 mL in 20–40 s in a calm cat. COMMON ERROR: normal-dog suction → instant collapse + a pink (hemolyzed) sample from a perfect stick. RECOVERY: ease off entirely for 2 s, resume at half pressure. STOP: the cat's breathing changes — the holder should be watching the face and calling it; you watch the hub.
**Exit.** Identical order to CSC-VEN-01 — occlusion off, needle out, pressure — with one feline addendum: hold pressure a full 60–90 s (thin wall, easy bruise) and re-wrap the leg INTO the towel afterward so the cat doesn't kick the clot loose.

## 7. Tactile medicine
The medial saphenous under your fingertip: wider and flatter than a cephalic — a ribbon, not a cord — with faint spring. Entry: almost no resistance; if you felt a real "push," you're too deep. Collapse: arrives earlier and at lighter suction than any dog vein — recalibrate your thumb to feline scale. The leg in your cupped hand telegraphs everything: a stiffening gastrocnemius is the 1-second warning before the kick.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**Visible vein, no flash:** you're beside it (angle drifted) or over it (too shallow even for this). Withdraw to skin, re-sight along the ribbon, redirect once — same attempt. **Flash then stop:** flatten to almost skin-parallel, advance 0.5–1 mm, quarter-turn, halve your suction. If dry: holder releases/reapplies occlusion. Two cycles = attempt over. **Immediate hematoma:** thin wall blown; pressure, other leg; log it honestly. **The cat "wakes up" mid-draw:** you have ~5 s of grace — decide NOW: 1 mL in the syringe may be enough for the priority test (ask before re-sticking for the rest); a thrashing cat ends the event. **Both hindlimbs spent/inaccessible:** the answer is a plan change (cephalic with a second towel plan, jugular by senior staff, or sedation per DVM) — not heroics. Hand off with the useful sentence: sites tried, what you felt, what's untouched.

## 9. Real patients
**Kittens:** vessel is thread; butterfly + 0.5 mL expectations; warm the leg first; volume math is critical — flag total draws >1% body weight in mL. **Geriatric/CKD cats** (you will draw hundreds): often dehydrated AND fragile — slow fill is normal; do not chase with suction; these cats' calm is worth more than the second tube. **Obese cats:** the ribbon hides in a fat pad — palpate at the tibia where fat is thinnest, extend the leg fully to stretch the pad flat. **Fractious flags:** towel-first ALWAYS, head hidden, room quiet, one attempt budget before regrouping — a fractious cat's second chance costs triple. **Hyperthyroid seniors:** ropey, mobile arteries nearby — if what you hit PULSES, exit immediately, firm pressure 2+ min, report (arterial sticks happen; hiding them is the sin).

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Dog-force restraint** — full-body pin "so it can't move." Consequence: the explosion you caused. Prevention: towel + hidden head + minimal points of contact. 2. **Dog-depth entry** — through-and-through on the first try. Prevention: rehearse the 10–20° sight-picture on the model. 3. **Dog-suction aspiration** — hemolysis machine. Prevention: syringe ≤3 mL, coin-lift thumb. 4. **Skipping the towel because "this cat's friendly"** — friendly cats un-friend at needle three; wrap first, always have the option live. 5. **Short post-draw pressure** — feline bruises are client-visible on the inner thigh; 60–90 s, then recheck.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: warm the leg through the towel while setting up — free 30% vein diameter. · Draw near the hock first; failures still leave the whole upstream ribbon. · The holder's voice matters more than their grip — a low steady murmur beats a tighter hand. · Schedule feline draws BEFORE anything else unpleasant in the visit; you get the best cat first. BANNED: scruff-and-stretch as the default hold (policy: towel first); "burrito tighter until it stops" (tighter wraps on a panicking cat = respiratory risk); blowing on the fur to part it (cats despise it; use alcohol's parting effect).

## 14. Technique variation & house standard
**Outcome requirements:** usable sample, cat still handleable at the end, no bruise, no sharps incident, and the cat's next visit not made worse.

**CVAH house standards** `[HOUSE]`: towel-first for flagged or unknown cats (reason: minimal-restraint policy, staff-injury history, and cats that stop being examinable); stop instantly on open-mouth breathing (reason: that is decompensation, not stress); pressure held long enough that the inner thigh doesn't bruise where the owner will see it.

**Genuinely variable here:** which end of the ribbon you work — hock-first is a preference, not a rule; burrito wrap direction; head covered versus uncovered (the cat votes); inguinal occlusion with flat fingers versus a thumb; sitting versus standing to work.

## 15. What may vary without being wrong
> Do not fail a learner for: working the vessel higher or lower along the tibia · a wrap that contains the cat by a different fold pattern · choosing the other hindlimb · a 25 g needle where the rubric imagined 23 g, or a butterfly instead of either · accepting a smaller volume and asking which test matters most · abandoning the site early because the cat's window closed — **that is judgment, and it should score well, not badly.**

## 16. Precision audit
`[GUIDE]`: 10–20° entry angle · the 60–90 second patience window · 1–3 mL syringe · 20–40 second fill · 60–90 second pressure.
`[HOUSE]`: towel-first for flagged cats · one-attempt budget on fractious patients before regrouping.
`[SAFETY]`: open-mouth breathing = stop · arterial stick = pressure and report, never conceal.
