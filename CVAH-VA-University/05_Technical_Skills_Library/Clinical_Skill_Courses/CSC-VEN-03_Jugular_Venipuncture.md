# CSC-VEN-03 — Jugular Venipuncture (Canine & Feline)
Companion to TS-VEN-JUG-001 · Benchmark course · ~25 min + supervised lab · S1 skill: your first live jugulars happen with a CVT/DVM at the table, always. Steps shared with CSC-VEN-01 not repeated.

## 1. Purpose
The jugular is the volume-and-quality vessel: big samples fast, minimal hemolysis, one stick for a full panel plus banked serum — and for kittens/tiny patients, often the only vessel with enough blood to matter. It's also the draw with the least room for sloppiness: the vessel lives next to the airway and the carotid, the patient's head is being held up and away, and a missed hematoma here can compress structures that matter. Respect, not fear.

## 2. Prerequisite knowledge
The jugular groove runs from the angle of the jaw down to the thoracic inlet (the notch where neck meets chest), on either side of the trachea. The vein lies IN the groove, superficial; the carotid artery is DEEPER and closer to the midline — a correctly superficial needle in the groove does not reach it. Occlusion is LOW: a thumb pressed into the groove just above the thoracic inlet dams the vein; it fills upward (toward the head) — so you stick ABOVE the thumb, needle pointing either direction per house convention `[CVAH-SPECIFIC INPUT REQUIRED: standardize head-ward vs chest-ward entry]`. Absolute assistant no-gos (escalate instead): suspected coagulopathy (rodenticide, low platelets — a neck hematoma can press the airway), respiratory distress (the position itself is dangerous), neck masses/wounds, and raised-intracranial/intraocular-pressure cautions per DVM.

## 3. Equipment
20–22 g needle; syringe sized to the FULL requested volume (6–12 mL — one continuous draw beats reloading); butterfly acceptable for cats/small dogs per trainer. Everything staged as ever; extra gauze — this site gets a real pressure hold after.

## 4. Patient preparation
Screening is part of the skill: before any jugular you personally confirm — no bleeding-risk flags in the record? breathing comfortable at rest? no cough-gag-stridor history that neck extension might provoke? If any doubt: ask FIRST. Position dog: sitting, rear tucked into the holder or a corner so reversing is impossible. Position cat: sternal at the table edge, forelimbs held down over the edge (drops the shoulders, straightens the groove), in or half-in a towel.

## 5. Operator preparation
- **Holder:** one hand cups the muzzle/chin from below and raises the nose toward the ceiling — smoothly, to maybe 45°, never cranked to maximum; the neck should look extended, not hyperextended. Other arm hugs shoulders/forelimbs down. The holder is ALSO the airway monitor: their eyes stay on nostrils and chest, and they own the stop-call.
- **You** stand/kneel at the patient's front, slightly to the draw side. Non-dominant thumb = the dam: pressed into the groove at the thoracic inlet, flat and firm. The same hand's fingers rest on the far side of the neck, steadying. Dominant hand: needle at 25–30°, in line with the groove.
- **Line of sight:** you're looking at a shaved-feeling gutter between two muscle bellies. In short-coated dogs with the head up, the filled jugular is often frankly visible — a soft ridge rising in the groove.

## 6. The procedure (unique steps)
**Raise.** DO: thumb dams the groove low; WATCH the groove fill from the inlet upward, 3–5 s. In doubt, strum test: lightly tap the ridge up high — a fluid wave transmits down a filled vein to your damming thumb. WHY: positive identification beats hope; the strum confirms vein (arteries don't wave, they pulse). EXPECT: a clear soft ridge in most patients; in fat necks, palpation only. COMMON ERROR: damming too high (mid-neck) — you're sticking into the unfilled segment. STOP: any breathing change with the nose raised — release the head NOW; that finding goes to the DVM before any retry.
**Confirm it's not the carotid.** DO: rest a fingertip on your target — a vein is soft, non-pulsatile, disappears when your dam lifts; a pulse = carotid = re-find your landmark shallower and more lateral. WHY: this 3-second check is the entire safety margin. COMMON ERROR: skipping it in visible-vein patients ("obviously the jugular") — the check is FOR the obvious days. STOP: can't convince yourself which structure you're on → don't stick; ask.
**Enter and draw.** DO: 25–30°, one smooth motion into the ridge above your thumb; flatten slightly on flash; aspirate steadily — this vessel TOLERATES a fuller pull than peripheral veins, that's its gift. EXPECT: brisk fill; 6 mL in seconds. COMMON ERROR: creeping needle advance while drawing (through the far wall mid-draw — flow stops and a deep bruise starts). RECOVERY: freeze your needle hand's position consciously; the syringe hand pulls, the needle hand is a statue. STOP: patient swallows/gags hard — pause the pull, let the swallow finish (the larynx moves — your needle shouldn't), resume only if geometry is undisturbed.
**Exit + the jugular-specific close.** DO: dam released → needle out → IMMEDIATE firm gauze pressure with the head returned to NEUTRAL (not still skyward) → hold a true 60–120 s → then look: lift gauze, watch the site through two breaths, palpate gently for a soft rising fullness. Recheck AGAIN before kennel return. WHY: this is the site where a lazy 10-second press buys a compressive hematoma. COMMON ERROR: pressure applied with the nose still up (the position that opened the vein keeps it open). STOP: any fullness growing under your fingers → continuous pressure, call the CVT/DVM now, do not leave the patient.

## 7. Tactile medicine
The filled jugular: broad, soft, slightly bouncy ridge — bigger than any vessel you've felt so far; the strum wave is unmistakable once felt (train it on every willing patient during rounds, no needle involved). The carotid: a deep, cord-like THUMP under real fingertip pressure — feel one once, with your trainer, on purpose, and you'll never confuse them. Entry: skin give then an easy vessel give — this wall is substantial; the "float" is obvious. Swallowing telegraphs through your damming thumb a half-second early — the groove shivers.

## 8. It didn't work — now what?
**Groove won't fill:** dam too high, too light, or off the groove (common in fat necks — walk your thumb laterally in small steps, re-test); patient's head insufficiently raised (re-cue holder gently); genuinely flat = sick patient information → escalate. **No flash in a visible vein:** you're skating on top (angle up a touch) or beside (re-sight the line of the groove). One redirect from just-under-skin; then out. **Flash then stop:** the far-wall creep (see above) or a swallow moved the target: flatten, statue-hand, re-aspirate gently; one cycle only at this site. **Hit a pulse / bright pulsing blood:** exit immediately, FIVE full minutes of direct pressure by the clock, report immediately — arterial sticks are survivable events when pressured and reported, and neither when hidden. **Two attempts spent:** this site's rule is stricter in spirit: jugular attempts are expensive; your default after ONE confident failure is to offer the draw to the senior at the table (they're there — this is S1).

## 9. Real patients
**Cats:** the edge-of-table sternal setup is everything — shoulders dropped, groove straightened; expect a shorter patience window; the whole draw should be under 60 s. **Puppies/kittens:** the jugular is often the ONLY adequate vessel; tiny dam pressure suffices; volumes are precious and pre-calculated. **Brachycephelics:** neck extension narrows already-crowded airways — partial extension only, holder on high alert, lowest threshold anywhere for aborting the position. **Deep/fat necks (bulldogs, chow coats):** all palpation, strum test mandatory, and honest recognition that some necks are a senior-staff draw. **Coagulopathy-SUSPECTED (not just confirmed):** the answer is no. Peripheral veins compress; necks are where bleeding hides.

## 10. Common rookie mistakes
1. **Cranking the nose to the ceiling** — hyperextension closes airways and stresses patients; 45° and smooth. 2. **Damming mid-neck** and sticking below their own thumb. 3. **Skipping the pulse-check** on easy-looking veins. 4. **Creeping the needle** during the pull. 5. **Ten-second pressure** and a kennel return — the hematoma forms unobserved; the double-recheck exists for exactly this. 6. **Treating jugular attempts like cephalic attempts** — spending both attempts before offering it up, at the one site where the senior is already standing next to you.

## 11. Pearls & banned folklore
PEARLS: strum-test practice on calm patients during TPRs costs nothing and builds the finger-memory that makes first jugulars boring. · In cats, drawing the forelimbs over the table edge does more for your view than any amount of neck extension. · Talk the holder through nose angle in degrees ("halfway to the ceiling") — "up a bit" produces cranking. · Bank the habit: big syringe, one continuous pull, tubes ready — the jugular's whole advantage is one-stick completeness. BANNED: jugular draws on unscreened patients "because the peripherals are blown" (screening IS the skill); two-person neck-stretch on a struggling patient (stop, regroup); pressure "while carrying the patient back" (pressure happens at the table, timed, then rechecked).
