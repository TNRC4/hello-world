### LABX-001 | LID-M1 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: Most in-house "analyzer errors" trace back to:
A) Reagent lots aging past their performance window B) Analyzer software needing vendor updates C) Sample quality and handling upstream of the machine D) Power fluctuations corrupting the run mid-cycle
KEY: C — fix the preanalytic pipeline before blaming the box; A, B, D happen but rarely.

### LABX-002 | LID-M2 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: QC material runs prove that:
A) The analyzer reads knowns correctly before patient results are trusted B) The consumable lots on board were stored properly C) The operator's loading technique stays consistent D) The species reference intervals are up to date
KEY: A. B, C, D are things QC partially touches but not its claim.

### LABX-003 | LID-M2 | D2 | L3 | SC:Y
Q: Morning QC failed; a sick patient's chemistry is waiting. You:
A) Run it and interpret cautiously with the QC note attached B) Resolve QC first per SOP; the sample waits or reroutes to the reference lab C) Run it twice and report if the runs agree D) Run QC and the patient sample together to save time
KEY: B. A ships numbers the machine just proved unreliable; C mistakes precision for accuracy; D gets an answer whose validity arrives too late to matter.

### LABX-004 | LID-M3 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: The hematology analyzer flags "review smear." You:
A) Rerun — flags often clear on the second pass B) Report the numbers with the flag noted C) Accept the numeric results; flags are advisory D) Prepare a quality smear for human eyes per the flag
KEY: D — the machine is asking for morphology it cannot do. A launders the flag; B and C pass the question downstream unanswered.

### LABX-005 | LID-M1 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: Chemistry on a grossly hemolyzed sample:
A) Runs fine — modern analyzers correct for sample color B) Skews several analytes — flag it and ask about redraw before running C) Only affects the CBC, so the chemistry is safe D) Is acceptable when the patient is genuinely hard to draw
KEY: B. A credits a correction that only partially exists; C aims the artifact at the wrong test; D negotiates chemistry with sympathy.

### LABX-006 | LID-M4 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: An analyzer error code appears. First stop:
A) The registry job aid / manufacturer error table, then log code and action B) A power-cycle restart — most transient codes clear on reboot C) The DVM, who decides how all equipment issues are handled D) The manufacturer support line, before anything else is touched
KEY: A. B may work and teaches nothing (and hides recurrence); C escalates a lookup; D skips the two-minute self-serve step support will ask about anyway.

### LABX-007 | LID-M3 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: Sediment-analyzer results versus manual microscopy:
A) The analyzer's read is the final word once QC has passed B) Manual microscopy always overrides the analyzer's call C) Certain findings trigger manual confirmation per SOP — know the trigger list D) Whichever method matched the last patient better wins
KEY: C. A and B pick a permanent winner in a case-by-case protocol; D is superstition.

### LABX-008 | LID-M2 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: Maintenance logs matter because:
A) The manufacturer requires them for billing B) Result validity and warranty support both rest on documented history C) They fill the quiet parts of bench shifts D) Inspectors read them before anything else
KEY: B. A, C, D are cynical or trivial versions of the real stake.

### LABX-009 | LID-M1 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: A refrigerated SNAP-style kit is used:
A) Straight from the fridge — cold preserves the reagents B) After equilibrating to room temperature per the insert C) After warming in your palm for thirty seconds D) At any temperature if the control spot develops
KEY: B. A and C bracket the insert's instruction with improvisations; D uses the control to excuse the protocol.

### LABX-010 | LID-M4 | D2 | L3 | SC:N
Q: The same error code returns after every per-SOP fix. You:
A) Keep cycling the fix — intermittent codes usually resolve B) Route samples to the backup path and escalate with the code history to support C) Recalibrate the analyzer to its last known-good settings D) Note the code and continue — the results look plausible
KEY: B. A defines insanity for analyzers; C is not an operator-level action; D lets plausible-looking numbers vouch for a failing instrument.

### LABX-011 | LID-M3 | D2 | L1 | SC:N
Q: Daily microscope care includes:
A) Alcohol-wiping every optical surface until it squeaks B) Removing the mechanical stage for a full cleaning C) Lens paper on optics, oil wiped after use, lamp down, cover on D) Disassembling the condenser weekly for deep cleaning
KEY: C. A degrades lens coatings; B and D are maintenance-tech tasks.

### LABX-012 | LID-M1 | D3 | L4 | SC:N
Q: Glucose reads 38 mg/dL on an afternoon run of a 9 AM sample; serum never separated; patient bright and normal. Most likely:
A) True hypoglycemia — alert the DVM so treatment starts now B) Analyzer drift through the day — rerun after a fresh QC C) In-vitro glucose consumption by cells over six hours — report the circumstances with the result D) A sample swap — some other patient's tube wearing this label
KEY: C — the classic delayed-separation artifact; the DVM gets the number AND its story. A treats an artifact; B blames the machine for the bench's delay; D leaps to a swap without evidence.
