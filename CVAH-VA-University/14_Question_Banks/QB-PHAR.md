# QB-PHAR — Pharmacy & Medication Handling Academy (18 items)
Rewritten to Assessment_Item_Quality_Standard 2026-08.

### PHAR-001 | PHA-M1 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: Verifying drugs by generic name matters because:
A) Generic products are more reliable B) The same molecule wears multiple brand names on one shelf C) Brand names change concentration more often D) Labels list generics in larger type
KEY: B. A, C, D are invented properties; the aliasing problem is the point.

### PHAR-002 | PHA-M2 | D2 | L3 | SC:Y
Q: The order reads "give 0.5 of dexamethasone." What blocks preparation?
A) Nothing blocks it, if the house stocks only one concentration B) The route is missing from the written order C) Unit and concentration are unresolved — query the prescriber D) The indication and duration aren't stated anywhere
KEY: C. A is the dangerous rationalization (stock changes; orders outlive shelves); B may also be missing but isn't the ambiguity named; D is never required on an order.

### PHAR-003 | PHA-M3 | D2 | L3 | SC:N
Q: You need 10 mg from a 100 mg/mL vial — 0.1 mL. Best practice:
A) Draw 0.1 mL carefully in a TB syringe and give it B) Prepare a verified dilution per protocol, labeled with the final concentration C) Ask the DVM to round the dose up to a measurable volume D) Use two 0.05 mL draws for accuracy
KEY: B — tiny volumes carry huge relative error. A is confident and error-prone; C reverses who serves whom; D doubles the error source.

### PHAR-004 | PHA-M4 | D2 | L1 | SC:Y
Q: Controlled-substance log entries happen:
A) At withdrawal, witnessed, balanced in the moment B) Within the hour, while memory is fresh C) At shift end, batched for accuracy D) When the reverse count is done weekly
KEY: A — real-time or wrong; B, C, D are the three drift cadences audits catch.

### PHAR-005 | PHA-M4 | D2 | L3 | SC:Y
Q: The controlled cabinet count is one tablet short. You:
A) Recount, then adjust the log if the recount agrees B) Report it immediately per policy C) Check whether yesterday's entries explain it, then decide D) Note it for the manager's Monday review
KEY: B. A "fixes" a discrepancy (the cardinal sin); C investigates alone before reporting; D schedules an urgent thing.

### PHAR-006 | PHA-M5 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: Hazardous-drug handling (cytotoxics and listed agents) requires:
A) Standard exam gloves with prompt handwashing afterward B) Handling restricted to the DVM personally C) Per-drug PPE from the hazardous list, with pregnancy-relevant flags D) Double-bagging the dispensed product for the client
KEY: C. A under-protects; B over-restricts (trained staff handle with controls); D is packaging, not protection.

### PHAR-007 | PHA-M2 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: A refrigerated med has sat out for an unknown time. You:
A) Return it to the fridge — cold chains tolerate brief gaps B) Use it today only, then discard C) Discard it without documentation D) Flag and report it — stability is now unknown
KEY: D. A assumes the gap was brief (unknown means unknown); B invents a half-life for the problem; C acts correctly but destroys the record inventory needs.

### PHAR-008 | PHA-M3 | D2 | L3 | SC:N
Q: 3 mg/kg for a 28 lb dog; 25 mg scored tablets. The defensible fill:
A) 38 mg ≈ 1.5 tablets — dispense if the order permits halves; confirm rounding with the prescriber B) 2 whole tablets — rounding up keeps the dose covered C) 1 whole tablet — rounding down is the conservative choice D) 1.5 tablets with no confirmation — a 2% variance never needs one
KEY: A — show the math, confirm the rounding policy. B and C round by temperament instead of policy; D does the math and skips the authorization.

### PHAR-009 | PHA-M1 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: "q8h" versus "TID":
A) Identical — both simply mean three times daily B) q8h fixes the interval; TID may compress into waking hours — confirm house convention C) TID is the stricter of the two and overrides q8h D) q8h is hospital dosing; TID is the take-home version
KEY: B. A erases a real difference that matters for antibiotics; C inverts it; D invents a setting rule.

### PHAR-010 | PHA-M6 | D2 | L3 | SC:Y
Q: A client calls: their pet vomited twice after starting the new medication. You:
A) Tell them to stop the med until the DVM calls back B) Suggest giving it with food going forward C) Take details, promise no advice, route to the DVM promptly, log the ADR report D) Reassure them — GI upset is common and usually passes
KEY: C. A and B are both prescribing acts in disguise (stopping and modifying); D counsels an outcome you can't promise.

### PHAR-011 | PHA-M2 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: The expiry sweep finds a fast-moving med expiring this month. You:
A) Quarantine or flag per policy, note for reorder, document disposal when due B) Move it to the shelf front so it dispenses through in time C) Extend it thirty days under the usual informal grace period D) Discard it today so expired stock never gets touched
KEY: A. B is retail logic in a pharmacy; C invents a grace period; D destroys usable stock early and skips documentation.

### PHAR-012 | PHA-M6 | D2 | L3 | SC:Y
Q: Your label-vs-vial check catches a strength mismatch before dispensing. This event is:
A) A caught near-miss — correct it AND log it B) Proof the system works; no record needed C) A printer issue for the office manager D) Reportable only if the client noticed
KEY: A — the near-miss data is how the system learns. B, C, D all discard the signal after enjoying the save.

### PHAR-013 | PHA-M3 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: 2 mL BID means a daily volume of:
A) 2 mL B) 4 mL C) 6 mL D) 8 mL
KEY: B. A forgets the second dose; C and D apply TID/QID math.

### PHAR-014 | PHA-M3 | D2 | L3 | SC:N
Q: A 14-day course at 0.75 mL BID needs a minimum bottle of:
A) 10.5 mL B) 14 mL C) 21 mL D) 30 mL
KEY: C — 1.5 mL/day × 14. A is the SID slip; B counts days as mL; D pads without a basis.

### PHAR-015 | PHA-M5 | D1 | L1 | SC:N
Q: Light-sensitive medications are stored:
A) In any closed cabinet B) In original or amber packaging per label C) Wrapped in foil as a universal fix D) Refrigerated, since cold protects from light
KEY: B. A assumes closed means dark; C substitutes an improvisation for the label; D swaps in the wrong protection.

### PHAR-016 | PHA-M4 | D2 | L2 | SC:N
Q: "Beyond-use" dating applies when:
A) The manufacturer's printed expiry date has passed B) A product is reconstituted or compounded — mark date/time and the shorter window C) A multi-dose vial has been in the fridge over 30 days D) The label has faded past confident readability
KEY: B. A is ordinary expiry; C invents a universal fridge rule; D is a labeling problem.

### PHAR-017 | PHA-M1 | D2 | L3 | SC:N
Q: A client asks for "the same antibiotic as last time — it worked great." You:
A) Fill it from the record; documented history authorizes a refill B) Fill half the course to bridge until the DVM reviews C) Route the request to the DVM; prepare only after authorization D) Suggest they try the OTC equivalent meanwhile
KEY: C. A converts history into prescription authority; B prescribes a smaller crime; D recommends therapy outright.

### PHAR-018 | PHA-M6 | D3 | L4 | SC:Y
Q: The strongest single defense against look-alike/sound-alike dispensing errors:
A) Hiring experienced staff who know the shelf layout cold B) Stocking a single brand per molecule to remove aliases C) Layered forcing functions — shelf separation, TALL-man labels, concentration read-alouds, never identifying by location D) A deliberate slow-down protocol during the busiest periods
KEY: C — systems beat vigilance. A and D are vigilance in different outfits; B helps but collapses at the first backorder substitution.
